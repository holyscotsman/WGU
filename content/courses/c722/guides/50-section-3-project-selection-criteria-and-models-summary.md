# Section 3: Project Selection Criteria and Models Summary

## Section 3: Project Selection Criteria and Models Summary

In this section, you explored how organizations select which projects to undertake, assuming that there are multiple projects from which to choose and that the company has limited resources that can be invested in projects. The decisions about which projects will move forward into planning and execution often occur prior to the project manager being involved.

As a project manager, it is important that you understand the models, methods and artifacts as described with PMBOK 7, which are equivalent with the processes, tools, and techniques used to evaluate and select projects.

You began by seeing that multiple projects may be selected in order to develop a program (set of related projects) to meet a company objective or serve a customer. You saw the example of an automobile manufacturer that initiates multiple projects under two programs that focus on the full-size car category and the SUV category. Portfolios were illustrated as projects across a company that all contribute to the overall organizational goal of cost reduction.

The use of models was introduced as a way to evaluate proposed projects. Models are abstractions that focus on specific aspects of reality: blueprints, physical models, artist’s conceptions, and equations. When choosing which models to use in project selection, it is recommended that the models have the following characteristics: be realistic, capable, flexible, easy to use, low cost, and comparable.

Four non-numeric models were introduced: competitive necessity, operating necessity, sacred cow, and checklist. These methods each meet the criteria listed above but do not evaluate the profitability or cost aspects of the proposed projects. To address the financial aspects of project proposals, numeric models were introduced.

Payback period evaluated the time required for a company to recover their initial investment in the project. Net present value (NPV) evaluated the net cash flows over the entire life of the projects and discounts of those future benefits based on the time value of money using a discount rate. Finally, internal rate of return (IRR) was introduced to evaluate a company’s investment in a project as if it were a stock investment to determine the rate of return on the investment in project costs.

You examined two types of scoring models in order to illustrate how multiple criteria can be integrated. The simple scoring model lists individual criteria and requires the evaluator to rate each on a scale of 1 to 5. The scores for each proposed project are added based on the score assigned to each criterion. Project selection priorities are then assigned based on each project’s aggregate score.

Recognizing that all criteria are not equal, you saw weighted factor scoring models that allow a company to place emphasis (weight) on some criteria over others. In these models, the evaluator’s score of a criterion is multiplied by a preassigned weight before the project proposal’s aggregate score is calculated.

This introduction to the project selection process provided several examples of non-numeric and numeric models, showed you how they are used, and gave you the opportunity to use them.
