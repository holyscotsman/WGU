# S5 1.2 Developing the Project Scope

## Learning Objectives

Describe how the scope of a project is developed.

## Project Scope

Over the prior sections, you have come to understand projects support the execution of organizational strategies and the advancement of business objectives. The goal of any project is to provide business value by developing new products or services, solving problems, or fixing defective features. There can also be situations where projects deliver multiple outcomes that stakeholders may value differently.

The delivery performance domain addresses activities and functions associated with delivering the project scope and quality that the project was undertaken to achieve.

Besides the delivery performance domain, you will see, developing the project scope will integrate several performance domains, such as the following:

## Team Performance Domain

Developing an effective scope plan requires the project team to foster a collaborative culture with key stakeholders. Productive engagement with stakeholders is essential, regardless of diversity and even when communication is virtual. The team must create a safe, nonjudgmental space that encourages transparency, integrity, respect, positive dialogue, support, and the courage to offer suggestions or dissent during the scope planning process.

## Development Approach and Life Cycle

Scope planning differs considerably in predictive and adaptive development approaches, and the key factor that differentiates them is the uncertainty associated with requirements. In a predictive approach, the scope can be defined with less ambiguity at the start of the project. Consequently, a detailed scope plan would be possible, and the scope plan would help define schedule, cost, and risks. When uncertainty and risks are associated with defining requirements or volatility exists in requirements, an adaptive (incremental or iterative) development approach is preferred. This approach would help narrow down requirements and investigate various options.

## Planning

The project deliverables influence scope planning. In a predictive planning approach, it is possible to define high-level deliverables and decompose them via a WBS down to an activity level.

## Measurement

Scope planning should identify the requirements clearly. Completion of requirements can be assessed using acceptance criteria, technical performance measures, and "definition of done." Baseline parameters are used in a predictive development approach for measurement. Promised business value and benefits are measured in an adaptive strategy.

## Uncertainty

Uncertainties associated with requirements can hinder the development of an effective scope plan. Uncertainty, volatility, complexity, and risk are some of the factors that, if associated with requirements, would compel the project team to employ an adaptive approach. A predictive approach aims to further minimize uncertainty with a detailed project and risk management plans.

The project scope was described earlier as one of the three components of the triple constraint. You focused on the statement of work (SOW) because it defines the project’s outcomes in terms of objectives, specific deliverables, acceptance criteria, technical requirements, milestones, constraints, and assumptions. Recall that the project charter's creation in the defining phase authorizes work on the project and gives a high-level view of the product, service, or result of the project. The charter does not generally include detailed descriptions of the work that actually will be done. The SOW is the document that clarifies the actual work to be done.

In addition to the SOW, the project scope (or scope statement) will also document the customer’s expectations regarding to when the project will be completed (the time/schedule constraint) and how much the completed project will cost (the budget or cost constraint). The scope statement becomes the standard by which project results will be measured. Projects can fail because the scope statement did not clearly, completely, and accurately capture the customer’s expectations. The project team will plan and execute the project based on the scope statement, so if the approved scope statement includes errors, ambiguities, or omissions, there is a high likelihood that the customer will not be satisfied with the project outcome.

The process of capturing customer expectations and then managing the scope statement occur throughout the life of the project. During the defining phase, an initial scope is created to support the project selection process and create the project charter. The scope statement is developed with additional details and formalized during the planning phase. Changes to the scope can be identified during the execution phase but must be carefully reviewed and approved before the scope statement can be updated. At project closure, the project deliverables are compared to the scope statement to determine whether the outcomes meet stated expectations.

The project scope is the standard by which project results will be measured. A process flowchart is shown, and the first step is the initial scope statement, which comes under the defining phase. The second step is approving the scope statement, which comes under the planning phase. The third step is the scope management process, which comes under the executing phase, and the fourth step is scope verification, which comes under the closing phase.

## Product Scope and Project Scope

The term product scope is used to describe the portion of the scope statement that defines the features and functions of the project outcome or deliverables. This is often equated to the "what" of project management, meaning "what will the project create?" If your project is to launch a new restaurant, then the product scope would describe the physical aspects of the restaurant such as the following:

size of the kitchen

specific cooking equipment number of tables

number of chairs

waiting area size

furnishings

lighting

computer equipment

The project scope describes how the project outcomes will be created. This description includes both what work will be done and the method that will be used. The project scope also includes the project management functions required to plan, coordinate, and manage the project work.

In the restaurant example, you know that plumbing work will be required to support many parts of the project scope to deliver quality food in a sanitary environment, specifically in the kitchen and restrooms. The method you use to complete that work could be to use a general contractor who would be responsible for selecting the plumber. The general contractor would oversee the work and be responsible for ensuring that the work supported the product scope, and he would also pay the plumber. An alternate method of completing the work would be for you to select your own plumber and electrician, oversee the work yourself, and pay the plumber and electrician directly.

Because the general contractor would expect to be paid for his work related to the plumber, you might save money by overseeing the plumber yourself. However, you would need the contacts and expertise required to engage a plumber and oversee the work. The decision of how the plumbing work will be managed would need to be described in the project scope statement to ensure that the stakeholders know what work is required and the impacts to budget and time. In addition, you must recognize that hiring the plumber and electrician yourself adds a layer of complexity to communications, coordination, and scheduling. If the general contractor hires these craftsmen, he is responsible for scheduling, etc. If you hire these people directly, you must schedule and coordinate their work in relation to other work that the general contractor is performing. This is one of the trade-offs that must be considered, similar to any make/buy decision in project management.

Both the product scope (description of the project outcomes) and the project scope (description of how the work will be completed) are included in the project scope statement. Organizations vary significantly in how they apply these two terms, with many not using the term product scope at all. Instead, the term project scope is used generically to include both the "what" and the "how."
