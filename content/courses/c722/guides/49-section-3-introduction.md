Project selection is the process of identifying which project(s) an organization will undertake. Assuming a company has many project proposals from which to choose, the selection process involves both evaluating and prioritizing the project proposals. If the company has sufficient resources, then it can adopt a set of projects.

As you may recall, a group of related projects is a program. This often occurs when a new product development is too massive to be undertaken in a single project. The product development will be subdivided into several projects which, taken together, constitute a program.

A group of projects, or programs, undertaken by a company in order to achieve strategic business goals is called a portfolio. The term portfolio is used in project management in much the same way as in finance. The company is investing its resources in a set of projects that vary in cost, benefit, and risk (like a stock portfolio) such that the group of projects together can be leveraged to maximize company benefit.

To clarify, you will look at two examples.

An auto manufacturer has a goal of increased market share in the luxury segment. The decision is to focus on two product lines: full-size passenger cars and SUVs. All components of these vehicles that are visible to the customer will be unique in appearance from any other vehicle. Each of these vehicles will be a separate program. Each of these programs will be further divided into projects. So there are separate projects, for instance, for front passenger seats, second-row seats, instrument panels and displays, steering wheels, door trim, etc. The separate projects together make up a program—one program for the passenger car and one for the SUV. And in this example, there are two programs that are part of a portfolio to attain the goal of increased market in the luxury segment.

Deciding which projects to undertake can be difficult since each one varies in its costs, benefits, and risks. To guide the decision-making process, companies often use a systematic framework or model(s) that can focus the decision maker on key aspects of the proposals. Which frameworks and models to use depends on the goals of the company. Companies want to select projects that fit within their capabilities and help them reach their goals. In project management, the term alignment is used to describe connecting project outcomes to company goals.

If you consider the opening of a restaurant as a complex undertaking that would need to integrate several projects, the execution of the business plan would be a program, and you can have several projects running within the program. In this example, your projects could be as follows:

When the sponsor (in this case, you, the owner) decides to start a restaurant, the first thing to do is to set a budget for the start-up and to understand financing sources. In the case of a small business, usually, the owner and friends and family put up the initial funds. These are limited and so, when setting up the program, the projects are often broken down based on what needs to be done for initial opening and what can be done later. Soon you may have more than four projects, and maybe you will do only part of the project before you launch the restaurant and do the rest as cash flow or investment allows. This will happen to accommodate cost constraint (one of the triple constraints) limitations.

It is also possible that you need to open the restaurant quickly to beat the competition. Timing is essential, so you need to divide up the program into projects that can be building blocks. This reflects a change to your time constraint. For example, use a smaller menu or do not open up the outside sitting area until later. These will then become other projects within the program to open the restaurant. This speaks to time and scope, which are the other legs of the triple constraint.
