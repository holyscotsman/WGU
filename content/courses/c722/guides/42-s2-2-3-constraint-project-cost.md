# S2 2.3 Constraint: Project Cost

## Learning Objectives

Define the project term triple constraint.

## Project Cost

The project cost is the sum total of all expenses directly charged to the project. This can include, but is not limited to the following:

- salaries and wages
- materials

facility purchases or rentals (to the extent the facility is used for the project) equipment purchases or rentals (to the extent the equipment is used for the project) travel expenses (for project-related business travel) technology investment including systems and processes and even the project management software cost of quality level all functions and scope requirements Certain business expenses are not typically considered part of the project and should not be charged to the project: Sometimes, additional permanent personnel must be hired to work on aspects of the project. If there are hiring costs for these personnel, such as recruiter fees, these fees are not typically considered project costs. Temporary personnel hired specifically for the project will have all their hourly rates charged to the project. There are occasions where a customer will request several bids from prospective contractors before project approval. The costs incurred by the contractor(s) in preparing any bid are not typically considered a part of the project cost. It is the cost of doing business or cost of business (COB) or the cost of sales for the contractor. There may be the issue of purchasing, building, or leasing of equipment or facilities. This may not be an option. Some project scope statements may require certain equipment to be purchased. In this case, these costs are chargeable to the project. If, however, equipment is

purchased with the intent of being available following the completion of this project, the purchase price is not chargeable to the project, but the operating costs are. The key to creating a budget is careful and complete documentation of the assumptions that come from the project-planning phase. If you gather information during planning, then creating the budget can be a mathematical process of multiplying cost by volume (hours, number of things purchased, etc.). A great tool to use for this can be lessons learned from a previous, similar project. Remember, any time there is a possible change in cost, it must be viewed regarding to any decrease in schedule or increase in scope. In other words, does the benefit balance the cost change?

Your desire is to have the benefit at least equal to the cost, but ultimately it is to drive greater profits for the business. If you simplify the concept, you have basic accounting:

- Sales – Cost = Profit

When you do a project, it should either increase sales or reduce costs to yield greater profits. Generally, reasons for justifying a project can be classified (not in priority order) as follows:

government requirements, such as environment; reduced emissions increased safety reduce costs/increase profits improved operating efficiencies improved manufacturing processes meet customer needs address competition While there may be many other justifications, most will fit into one of these key categories.
