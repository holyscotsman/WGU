# S5 4.1 Estimation Techniques

## Learning Objectives

Describe the different types of estimation techniques.

## Methods for Estimating Project Costs

Cost and time are two of the three triple constraints introduced earlier in this course. Cost and time are closely related because the time required to complete a work activity will dictate the labor costs associated with the activity. Time and costs associated with suppliers, facilities, or equipment required to complete a work activity must be considered along with internal labor and resources. As a project manager you cannot work with cost without considering time, and both are driven by the third constraint—scope. Initial estimates of cost, often termed "rough costs," are necessary at the proposal phase of a project in order for the organization to determine if the project should be approved for planning and execution. These early approximations of cost are most often developed using top-down estimation methods. As the term suggests, these estimates are often provided by someone at the organization who has knowledge or experience from prior projects similar to the project being considered. This is often documented by previous lessons learned. While the knowledge of the experienced estimator cannot be guaranteed, these estimates provide a starting point for the project. Bottom-up estimation methods require estimates at the detailed work activity level of the WBS. These estimates are generated by functional experts with knowledge of the resources required and available to complete the activity. The individual work activity estimates are aggregated to the project level to develop the overall project budget. Due to the project expertise needed and effort required to develop bottom-up estimates, these are normally developed during the detailed planning phase of a project when the knowledge is greater than at the start of the project.

## Top-Down Methods: Ratio and Apportion

These top-down methods are used at the defining phase of a project. Early in a project’s life cycle, the organization is determining how the project will meet a business need, what the cost will be, and how long the project will take to complete. If the project is approved, the WBS will be developed, and a better understanding of the specific work activities will allow for more detailed estimating. When these top-down methods are based on the relationship between the current project and historical data, they are often referred to as parametric estimating. Two applications of parametric estimating are the ratio and apportion methods. The ratio method uses experience from prior projects to estimate the overall cost of the current project. The ratio method can also be adopted for other project types where the historic costs can be equated to the current project. The development of a website with an expected number of web pages could be estimated based on the historic cost per page developed. Painting a restaurant’s dining room is a fairly standard job. The room is 20 feet by 30 feet. An experienced painter can estimate the amount of paint required and the labor needed based on experience painting similar rooms by figuring out the ratio of paint and labor used on other inside commercial jobs. The apportion method is based on the ratio method but takes into consideration specific functionality or types of work that will be required. The apportion method requires that an overall project cost be provided based on the customer’s budget or similar projects. The overall budget is then allocated to the major work areas required in the project (design, programming, testing, documentation, user training). Based on experience, the project organization may estimate that 20% of the total cost of the project will be needed for design, 30% for programming, 20% for testing, 15% for documentation, and 15% for user training.

The apportion method is often used as the project transitions from the defining to the planning phase. If the defining stage identified an overall cost estimate of $500,000, then there is an expectation that the project planning phase should return with a budget close to the expected $500,000. In the previous example, the apportion method would allocate 30% of the $500,000 estimate to programming. During the planning phase, the programmers would be challenged to respond whether the work identified in the WBS can be completed for $150,000 (30% of $500,000). If the programming cannot be completed for $150,000, then the amount and any changes to scope need to be negotiated in order to reach a feasible solution.

## Bottom-Up Methods

Bottom-up estimating methods literally start at the bottom level of the WBS and aggregate cost estimates up to the total project. Creating a bottom-up estimate requires that people with expertise in doing the work estimate the labor, supplies, materials, equipment, and any other resources needed to complete each individual work activity. The work activities are identified during the development of the WBS, so a bottom-up estimate cannot be created until the WBS is available. The costs of each work activity are then added to estimate the total cost of the project. A variation on the basic bottom-up method is the use of templates. Using templates can speed up the process of developing work activity estimates. If an organization has experience with similar work activities in the past, then that knowledge can be used to provide guidelines for the costs normally associated with that work activity. If the work activity is to pour the concrete floor for a standard garage, then the labor and materials required would be known from prior projects. Adjustments to the labor or materials could be made if colored concrete or special stamping is required for the project. This is very similar to the top-down ratio method, but notice how each of the work activities would be evaluated and adjusted individually before being aggregated up to the project level.
