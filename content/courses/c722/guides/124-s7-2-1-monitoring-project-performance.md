# S7 2.1 Monitoring Project Performance

## Learning Objectives

Describe how managers monitor against project baselines.

## Monitoring Project Scope

The project scope was originally defined during the initiating phase as part of the project charter. The initial scope was further refined during the planning phase by rigorously collecting, researching, and analyzing stakeholder requirements. The resulting scope statement was approved by the project team, project sponsor, and key stakeholders. At the conclusion of the planning phase, this scope statement became the baseline project scope, which means that it is the approved description of the project outcomes and deliverables that is to be used to measure project performance.

Now you are in the executing phase of a project. This is where the work of the project is performed. This also includes leading, managing, monitoring, and controlling all of the processes, principles, and performance domains simultaneously to ensure progress is being made and results will be delivered.

During the execution phase of the project life cycle, the project team will monitor project performance to ensure that the approved project scope will still be met. This is done through regular status reporting and meetings comparing activities and current knowledge to the baseline. Because the project scope was baselined at the conclusion of the project planning phase, the project manager will need to ensure that the baselined scope remains appropriate for the life of the project or is modified and approved through change control accordingly.

When differences between actual performance and the baseline scope are identified, the variance must be analyzed to determine whether a change in scope is warranted. Sometimes the variance is a result of the project plan not producing the desired outcomes. In this case, corrective action may be needed to revise the plan or otherwise adjust shortcomings in the deliverable. If corrective action or scope changes are identified, they are documented and reviewed prior to implementation.

A flowchart shows the steps involved in analyzing variances in project scope. In the first step, the project manager monitors the scope proposed by the team to check if it is appropriate for the life of the project. In the second step, the variances are analyzed to determine whether a change in scope is warranted. In the third step, if corrective actions or scope changes are identified, they are documented and reviewed prior to implementation. Two common situations occur that may prompt a change to the baseline scope. First, the scope may be expanded to include additional functionality. Second, the scope may be diminished due to changes in the project environment, such as reduced funding or requirements or changing time or due date.

The project scope may be diminished or have functionality removed during project execution. If the schedule or budget are running over, the scope may be decreased in order to finish on time or within cost. Recall that the triple constraints of scope, time, and cost require that at least one constraint will need to be decreased in order to maintain the other two in the event of changes or wrong estimates.

The decrease in the scope can be the result of internal or external conditions. If external, such as a competitor releasing products with additional features, then the project scope may need to be enhanced. If the project needs to be released to the market sooner, scope may need to decrease in order to deliver the project faster. Internally, opportunities may become available, or the plan may not have been sufficient to meet the original scope.

Scope creep occurs when the project team integrates enhancements to the scope without proper evaluation and approval. This can result in the team incurring costs or schedule changes that the customer is unwilling to pay for or that may put the project behind schedule. Project managers monitor the project scope to ensure that all changes are properly evaluated and approved in order to avoid scope creep. It is critical that the team be vigilant about scope creep and identifying change, no matter how small or seemingly insignificant.

Whenever enhancements or decreases are necessary or proposed, they must be fully documented, evaluated, and approved before implementation. The baseline scope will not be changed; however, the approved revisions will be noted so that the project manager can explain why the delivered scope varied from the baseline.

## Monitoring Project Schedule

Monitoring project schedule refers to comparing the actual work breakdown structure (WBS) activities completed to the schedule baseline. The completion status of each WBS work activity is part of work performance data. The work performance data will identify the work activities that are completed, partially completed, or not started.

For activities that have been completed, the analysis will compare the planned activity duration to the actual activity duration. For activities partially completed, the analysis will compare the planned duration, actual duration, duration remaining, and percent complete. For activities not yet started, the analysis will verify that the planned start date has not yet occurred and that its preceding activities are on schedule.

Monitoring a project schedule refers to comparing the actual work breakdown structure activities completed to the schedule baseline. The first step for monitoring the project schedule is monitoring the activity completion status. For completed activities, the analysis compares the planned activity duration to the actual activity duration. In the second step, the variances are analyzed. Variances may be caused by errors in estimating the activity durations. The third step is to propose changes to the schedule or plan. By analyzing the status of all activities, variances can be identified. Variances may be caused by errors in estimating the activity durations. This may require that future activity durations be re- estimated or that new resources are assigned to the activities to ensure that they are completed as planned. The analysis of variances may also identify that a change in the baseline budget is needed that will require additional analysis and the approval of the customer prior to implementation.

## Monitoring Project Costs

Monitoring project costs refers to comparing actual costs to the baseline budget. This occurs throughout the execution of the project so that any deviations from the budget can be identified and assessed.

Earlier, the course discussed the simple budget and the calendarized budget. The calendarized budget identified the project costs and when they would occur. When monitoring project costs, the project manager will compare actual costs to the calendarized budget. This will identify not only if actual expenses are different than the budget but also if the expenses are occurring in a time period different than planned.

Remember that the timing of expenditures can also be important because other parts of the organization are planning cash availability to pay expenses. When expenses occur earlier or later than expected, money may not be available to pay the invoices. Cash flow is a critical issue in most organizations.

A flowchart shows the monitoring project costs process. The first step is to monitor actual expenditures, which are compared to the baseline budget based on time period, activity, and total for the project. In the second step, the variances are analyzed. The variance analysis can result in a proposal to revise the project baseline budget. The third step is to propose changes to the budget or plan. If a change is proposed, then it will be evaluated and approved by the appropriate stakeholders before being implemented. While monitoring of the project costs occurs throughout the execution of the project, it does not occur constantly. Monitoring of costs is planned into the schedule as appropriate depending on the magnitude of the project. The plan may call for monitoring to occur based on time periods (weeks, months, quarters, etc.) or at milestones during the execution (the completion of specific activities or groups of activities).

Actual expenditures are compared to the baseline budget based on time period, activity, and total for the project. By comparing multiple levels, the source of variances can be clearly identified. The analysis of the variance can result in a proposal to revise the project baseline budget. If a change is proposed, then it will be evaluated and needs approval by the appropriate stakeholders before being implemented.

The actual work completed must be considered when analyzing variances. If the project team is completing work faster or slower than expected, then some expenditures may occur at a different time than anticipated. This will appear as a variance when comparing the calendarized budget to the actual expenditures.

The process of assessing the work completed is called earned value analysis (EVA). Using EVA allows the project manager to compare the actual WBS work activities completed to the actual expenditures. EVA is a powerful tool in project monitoring that will be discussed in detail in the next lesson.
