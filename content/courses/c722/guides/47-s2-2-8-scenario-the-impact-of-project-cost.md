# S2 2.8 Scenario: The Impact of Project Cost

## Learning Objectives

Describe how triple constraint impacts project quality.

## The Impact of Project Cost

Your initial cost estimates are completed, and you have a budget worked out. You have allocated cash and have arranged financing for the remainder. But (there is always a “but”), construction workers and transport drivers go on strike. The good news is they settle early enough that you can maintain your scheduled start date. The bad news is your costs are increasing. You do not have a lot more cash you are willing to use for this project. And you are not comfortable with a lot of increased financing. You do not want to compromise quality. You must look at revisions to scope and to schedule. Some things cannot be dropped from scope and cannot be postponed. These include the following:

- the house itself
- health and sanitation concerns
- environmental and safety regulations
- the access road

An access road must be completed to be able to bring in materials and labor. A graded, packed dirt road is adequate for this and could serve as your permanent access road. This is both the quickest and the least costly means of access. You decide to go with the dirt road. In can always be covered by a gravel or an asphalt top layer at a later date, if so desired, and funding allows. Water supply and waste disposal will also need to be completed early on. You prefer to connect to the county water and sewer systems, but this is expensive because you have to pay for lines to be run to the mains. The least expensive way to do this is to siphon water from the lake and have a residential water treatment system installed and to build a septic system for waste treatment and disposal. You install both of these with the ability to connect with water and sewer mains at a later date, if desired.

Connecting to the power grid is much more expensive than purchasing a generator and a fuel storage system. You decide to go that route because you really only need power when you are in occupancy. When the time comes and you live there full time, you can run the connections and have your generator as a backup. You find that it is less expensive to expand your three-car garage to also house your snowmobiles than to build a separate snowmobile shed. You decide to do that. You will postpone construction of a boathouse till a later date. You initially planned to furnish one room at a time. You negotiate with your furniture supplier. If you pay cash up front for all the furniture, he will reduce or eliminate delivery charges to your home. You weigh the time cost of money to pay him up front versus the savings on delivery costs. You can make a similar arrangement for delivery and installation of all your appliances. You review your revised scope. You decide that the features that you postponed are actually removals from the scope of this project. You will address them as separate projects when you do add them. All in all, you are satisfied that you maintained quality, the schedule is minimally impacted, the scope changes are not severe, and the costs do not rise significantly.
