# S1 1.1 The Importance of Projects in an Organization

## Learning Objectives

Define projects, including their purpose.

Organizations are complex entities that engage in two types of activities: operations and projects. Operations are the day-to-day activities in which organizations engage. The result of these activities might be a product or a service satisfying a need that a customer is willing to pay for. The other activity is a project that brings value and delivers benefits to an organization and its stakeholders by adding or improving products or services for future profitability. A project develops a new product or service, including new technology features, an improved service, or cost reduction for the business in providing services, such as better accounting systems. Organizations—and the operations and projects in which they engage—are becoming increasingly more complex due to a number of factors, including the following:

- change in workforce demographics

increase in use of technology for better productivity and reduced costs; results may include the following:

- workers able to participate from remote locations

increase in cross-cultural workforce and customer bases; results may include the following:

- increase in multinational organizations
- increase in worker migration throughout the world

movement of sections of the organization to more competitive locations increase in international activities increase in demand for newer and more complex products at lower prices and better quality decrease in product life cycles change in governmental laws, regulations, and reporting requirements increase in corporate social responsibility; results may include the following:

- concern for environmental issues
- increase in corporate ethical practices

All of these factors exacerbate the need for educated and experienced professionals to lead and manage projects. This has created a well-respected and well-compensated career path.

## History of Project Management

The study of management in general, and project management in particular, is a fairly recent scientific undertaking. Prior to the mid- to late-19th century, there were no professional managers. Companies were managed by their owners, and new managers were trained by old managers. This was adequate when organizations were (relatively) small and technology changed slowly. With the advent of larger corporations and rapidly changing technology, the need for professional managers, separate from company owners, became evident and essential. From this came the development of project management knowledge as a field of study. The timeline below highlights some of the key events in the history of project management.

## Scholars of Project Management

## Image is in the Public Domain

Frank and Lillian Gilbreth were the "parents" of what is now known as time and motion study. Frank Gilbreth was an early user of motion picture technology (still in its infancy at the time), which he used to capture and study workers' repetitive motion tasks.

Understanding the way tasks are performed is very important. Reduction of worker fatigue reduces time lost to accidents and injury. Knowledge of how long tasks take to perform is vital to developing project schedules. Use of movies to record how tasks should be performed provides valuable record retention and standardization. Following Frank’s death, Lillian continued the work they started, producing the book The Psychology of Management (1921). Their goal was to understand the processes that people went through while engaging in manual tasks, with the goal of reducing fatigue and injury. An outgrowth of their work was the development of standard procedures for doing repetitive tasks. These methods are still employed when estimating schedules and work breakdown structures.

Frederick Taylor is known as the father of scientific management.

## Image is in the Public Domain

Frederick Taylor is known as the father of scientific management. From the mid-1800s to the early 1900s, he used scientific reasoning to study human labor relative to organizational system goals. He determined that by breaking down work into individual discrete activities, one could improve both human productivity and, subsequently, organizational productivity. Using time and motion studies, Taylor applied this work across an entire factory system yielding improvements for the individual and organization. As with the work of the Gilbreths, these techniques are still used and still help with determining work breakdown structure, scheduling, and resource requirements.

## Henry Gantt, creator of the Gantt chart

## Henry Gantt, creator of the Gantt chart

## Image is in the Public Domain

In the late 1800s and early 1900s, Henry Gantt, an associate of Taylor, studied organizational operations. Specifically, he studied management techniques in the construction of navy ships used in the First World War. As a result of his studies, he created the Gantt chart, a horizontal bar chart that sequences tasks and provides the duration of each task. Since this invention, Gantt charts have been used by project managers. The advent of powerful computer software has increased the utility of the Gantt chart. Modern computer software enables functions such as dependencies, milestones (designating the completion of major project components and decision points), the calculation of critical path, and scheduling of resources required at different times in the project.
