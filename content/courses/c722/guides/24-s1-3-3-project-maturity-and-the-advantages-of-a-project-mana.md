# S1 3.3 Project Maturity and the Advantages of a Project Management Office (PMO)

## Learning Objectives

Explain the advantages of a project management office (PMO).

## Organizational Maturity Levels

In an organization with only one project that is not very complex, there is no need for a project management office (PMO). The reality, however, is that organizations execute multiple projects concurrently, and these projects are increasingly complex. You previously saw three types of organizational structures: functional, projectized, and matrix. In both the projectized and the matrix structures, project managers have SMEs assigned to work on their projects. Simply examining the organizational chart, you might get the impression that the need for SMEs remains constant over the life of a project. This is not the case. The number of SMEs, and the expertise required, change along the project life cycle. In addition, the need for other resources, such as facilities and equipment, varies during the project's life. There are five levels of project management maturity (Crawford, 2007):

- initial process
- structured processes and standards
- organizational standards and institutional processes
- managed process
- optimizing process

The Delaware Department of Technology and Information also uses five levels to define its Organizational Project Management Maturity Model (OPM3).

Each level is color-coded and includes descriptive bullet points explaining the characteristics of that stage. Level 1, labeled Ad Hoc, indicates the lowest maturity. At this level, there are no formal standards, processes, procedures, or resources in place that would constitute a defined project management discipline. Technologies and reporting methods are used sporadically and inconsistently, making project execution unpredictable. Level 2, known as Planned, reflects an organization that has some standards, processes, procedures, and resources, but they are not applied organization-wide. Basic documentation is present, but management support is inconsistent. As a result, project practices remain somewhat informal and lack uniform application. Level 3, or Managed, represents a more structured approach where standards, processes, procedures, and resources exist and are applied as organizational standards. Formal documentation is maintained, and management support is consistent. However, execution may still be applied irregularly or inconsistently across projects. Level 4, labeled Integrated, shows a further degree of refinement and alignment. In this stage, more advanced standards, processes, procedures, and resources are in place. Documents are more refined, management support is consistent, and execution is reliably implemented across projects. Organizations operating at this level demonstrate efficiencies in project execution and have metrics in place to collect and monitor performance data. Level 5, known as Sustained, is the highest maturity level. Here, lessons learned and best practices are systematically applied to continuously improve existing standards, processes, procedures, and resources. Metrics are consistently collected and applied across project, program, portfolio, and organizational levels. The organization is well-positioned to make informed, strategic decisions based on past performance, ultimately enhancing competitive performance. This model illustrates the evolution from informal, unstructured project management practices to a disciplined and datainformed environment that fosters continuous improvement.

## Determining an Organization's Project

## Management Maturity Level

An organization’s maturity level is determined by the comprehensiveness of written procedures to accomplish tasks in each of the process areas identified by the PMI. At the lowest level of maturity, Level 1, there may be an acknowledgement of the need for procedures, but there are few, if any, formalized written procedures in existence. Reporting is sparse, if it takes place at all. Procedures are determined by individual project managers and are not consistently carried out throughout the organization. There is progressive elaboration of the quantity and quality of procedures through Levels 2, 3, 4, and 5. Each of the process areas must be broken down into individual components (Crawford, 2007). Each component in each process area must be analyzed individually to determine its maturity level. The overall maturity level of the project management process is determined by the lowest maturity level of all the processes. So, for example, if all components of all process areas are at a Level 4, with the exception of one process rating at a Level 3, then the entire process is considered at Level 3.

## Benefits of Having a PMO

Not all organizations require the structure and discipline of a PMO. When determining the need for a PMO, stakeholders should ask the following questions:

- Do we have a budget available to fund it?
- How many concurrent projects do we deal with on a regular basis?
- What is our percentage of failed projects?
- Are we having to deal with repeated project management issues?
- What is our ratio of experienced to inexperienced project managers?

What is our level of need for centralized project monitoring and control? In today’s larger, more complex organizations, PMOs are increasingly important. The PMO does the following:

aligns project investment to corporate strategy monitors status and availability of all project resources keeps resources employed to best advantage across multiple projects reduces overhead and bureaucracy and creates economies of scale serves as a central repository for all project policies, procedures, and historical records serves as a repository for lessons learned and for ensuring best practices are carried across to all projects ensures consistent policies and procedures across projects
