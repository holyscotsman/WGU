# S6 1.1 Estimating Activity Durations

## Learning Objectives

Describe common techniques used to estimate activity duration.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) The process of estimating how long work activities will take to complete can begin when the work breakdown structure (WBS) has been developed. In project management, the term duration is often used to describe the number of time periods required to complete some portion of the work. To keep the estimation process manageable, you should often focus on the specific work activities required to complete the project deliverables. The lowest level of the WBS identifies the work activities or tasks—or at least a good attempt at a comprehensive list of activities. As the project team develops the activity duration estimates, they may divide activities or identify additional activities that need to be added to the WBS. The estimating process is iterative in nature because, as you move from one step to another, your knowledge grows and adjustments can be made. While this iterative process can be frustrating, it is a normal situation. It is critical that all work activities are identified and properly included in planning documents. A common statement in project planning is that if an activity is not identified, it will not be budgeted, it will not be scheduled, and it will not be executed. So the time the team takes to identify activities required to create the deliverables is during the planning phase, not during execution. As has been mentioned, activities are the starting point for developing the project schedule because they identify specific work that will need to occur to execute the project. They are best described using a verb-noun approach to clarify what is to be accomplished (e.g., edit documentation, install hardware, test user interface). The activity durations will be integrated with the activity sequencing to become the approved schedule, so it is important that the team member doing the work has input into the duration estimates. Recall that the project charter may already include the customer’s expectation of when the project will be completed. The detailed scheduling process may identify that the proposed schedule is not feasible. The sequenced activity durations will provide a feasible schedule that the project manager can take back to the customer for negotiating the delivery of the project. The triple constraint perspective suggests that if the scope cannot be achieved under the proposed budget and schedule, then at least one element of the three must be changed.

## Activity Duration Estimating Methods

Duration estimation techniques fall into two general categories: deterministic and probabilistic. Deterministic duration estimation techniques are appropriate when the activity durations are very predictable. Probabilistic duration estimation techniques are used when there is uncertainty in how long an activity will take. Both techniques require input from the people who will be doing the work. Using the deterministic approach requires that the team expert provide a reliable estimate of the time necessary to complete the activity. This method is also called the one-point estimate method because the expert provides a single estimate for an activity based on either his or her own knowledge or historical information. If the expert has extensive experience and nothing in this particular project introduces uncertainty to the activity, the deterministic approach can provide a reliable estimate. Unfortunately, the people providing the estimate can easily be too optimistic or too pessimistic, which results in unreliable duration estimates. Because it is easy to use, the one-point method is very popular in project management. However, be cautious when applying this technique because the net result of inaccurate activity duration estimates will lead to an overall project schedule that is not reliable and often not attainable. Probabilistic approaches use basic statistics to account for uncertainty in activity duration estimates. Do not worry if you cannot remember anything about statistics; the application of these two methods is very straightforward. The two examples provided here are both based on the three-point estimate. Essentially, the activity duration is based on the combining of three estimates rather than just one. The experts are asked to provide an optimistic estimate (O), a pessimistic estimate (P), and a most likely estimate (M).

## The Triangular Distribution

## The Triangular Distribution Formula

The first probabilistic approach is the triangular distribution or simple average. The three estimates are averaged together.

The simple average gives equal weight to all three estimates. In this case, the optimistic (O) and pessimistic (P) are treated equally with the most likely estimate (M). This represents a slight improvement over the single-point estimate because the best-case and worst-case estimates are included in the calculation of the activity duration estimate.

Apply the triangular distribution using an example. For a given activity, the project team member responsible for the activity has been asked to provide three estimates of its duration: an optimistic estimate, a most likely estimate, and a pessimistic estimate.

## Optimistic Estimate = 15 days

## Most Likely Estimate = 20 days

## Pessimistic Estimate = 35 days

The triangular distribution method arrives at an estimate of 23.33 or 23 days (rounded).

## The Beta Distribution

The second probabilistic method is the beta distribution or weighted average. The beta distribution still uses the three estimates (optimistic, most likely, and pessimistic) but places emphasis on the most likely estimate. The beta distribution method was developed as the project evaluation and review technique (PERT). As introduced earlier in the course, PERT was developed in the 1950s to support the U.S. Navy’s Polaris missile project, which was so revolutionary that there was great uncertainty in how long each activity would require.

## The Beta Distribution Formula

The formula for the beta distribution places emphasis on the most likely estimate. This is accomplished by multiplying the most likely estimate (M) by a factor of four. Notice in the formula that the denominator is increased to 6 to account for the emphasis on the most likely estimate.

This method has the benefit of incorporating uncertainty and placing emphasis on what is most likely to occur. An interesting aspect of the beta distribution is that the level of uncertainty can be specifically measured based on the range or the standard deviation.

The range is simply the difference between the best-case and worst-case estimates. A large range would suggest that there is greater uncertainty in the time that will actually be required to complete the activity.

The standard deviation is a statistical measure of how much the actual activity duration is likely to vary. Consider an example using the same three estimates from the triangular distribution:

- Optimistic Estimate = 15 days
- Most Likely Estimate = 20 days
- Pessimistic Estimate = 35 days

Based on the beta distribution, the activity duration estimate would be 21.67 or 22 days (rounded).

The same information can be used to calculate the activity standard deviation, which is 3.33 or 3 days (rounded). This would suggest that the activity estimate of 22 days can be expected to vary by +/− 3 days. By adding and subtracting the standard deviation with the activity duration estimate, we can predict that the actual activity duration will very likely be between 19 and 25 days.

Notice in the example that the activity durations are estimated in days. These would be work days, which the project host organization will often define as being 8 hours per day for 5 days each week. Recall from the development of the WBS that a good rule of thumb is to not divide

work into periods of less than one day. While there may be special situations where shorter activities may be identified, generally the work can be grouped such that activities can realistically span at least one day.

The duration estimates can be measured in hours, days, weeks, months, or whatever time period is appropriate for the project. In the top left and right corners, a monthly calendar and a stopwatch are shown. At the bottom left and right corner, an hourglass and a daily calendar are shown. Depending on the project, other time periods can be used for planning purposes. Other time periods include hours, weeks, months, and quarters. Whatever time period is selected, it must be used consistently across the project. It would not be beneficial to have some activities in a project estimated based on hours and others on weeks.

## Learning Curves

The concept of learning curves suggests that as a person repeats a task, the time required to complete the task decreases. More simply, the more often you do something, the better and faster you become. This concept, developed by Boeing during World War II, noted that the rate of improvement in aircraft assembly was so regular that it could be calculated in a mathematical formula, even though learning curve rates differed by industry.

Learning Curve is written on a chalkboard with light bulbs around it. patpitchaya/iStock / Getty Images Plus via Getty Images. This concept can be important for creating accurate activity duration estimates when the project includes tasks that are repeated. If a project requires that a software test be run several times during the development of the application, the time required to run the test may decrease later in the project. The rates that the duration decreases can vary. If a different person completes the activity each time, then little or no improvement may be anticipated. If the person completing the activity has extensive experience with the activity from prior projects, then the learning curve improvements may have already occurred. Other factors that can influence the impact of learning curves include the complexity of the activity, the level of human input required, the duration of the activity, and the skill level required.

If the person completing the activity will be repeating the activity later in the project, the effect of learning curves should be taken into consideration when estimating durations for future repetitions of the activity. Experience is a great teacher. The project manager and the person doing the work must establish the rate of improvement to expect and at what point the learning curve will stop affecting duration. Learning curves are often applied based on a percentage improvement (10%, 20%, 30%, etc.). If an activity is initially estimated to have a duration of 10 days and the team anticipates that a second repetition of the activity will result in a 20% improvement, then the duration estimate for the second occurrence would be eight days. A third repetition may provide an additional 10% improvement making its duration 7.2 days. As you can see, the rate of improvement and how many repetitions of the activity experience the learning curve effect must be determined by the project manager and the people doing the work. A caution here is that the person creating the estimate needs to document how they have accounted for the learning curve. Their assumptions regarding how experience is included in the estimates will affect how the learning curve is incorporated into project planning and experienced during execution.
