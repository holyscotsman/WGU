# S4 1.1 Project Management Methods

## Learning Objectives

Contrast common project management methods.

## Project Management Methods

At this point, you are familiar with the PMBOK 7th edition principles and performance domains since this course follows it very closely. PMBOK is not technically a method for project management, but it is officially a standard for project management that deals with many accepted methodologies. As a standard, PMBOK identifies best practices in project management which can be applied across a range of projects and environments. Many elements and tools of project management discussed in this course can be found in the traditional project management methods and in the PMBOK standard. The Development Approach and Life Cycle performance domain is one of the eight performance domains as described by PMBOK. This performance domain is very important to project management as it involves defining the development approach, setting the delivery cadence, and determining the project life cycle to enhance project results. This domain also interacts with other domains such as the Stakeholder, Planning, Uncertainty, Delivery, Project Work, and Team performance domains. The development approach to creating and developing the product, service, or result throughout the project life cycle can vary. Methods include predictive, iterative, incremental, agile, or hybrid approaches. It is important to establish a clear understanding of these terms and concepts and how they play a part in the development approach and life cycle performance: When referring to a deliverable, this is a distinct and verifiable output, such as a product, result, or service capability, that must be produced to complete a process, phase, or project. A project phase refers to a group of logically related project activities that end with completing one or more project deliverables. The project life cycle comprises a series of phases that a project undergoes from inception to completion. The selected life cycle impacts how planning is carried out. It should reflect the characteristics of the project and the frequency of required results and outputs. This decision is

essential for optimizing the outcomes and value delivered to an organization. The project cadence is important, as it illustrates the sequence of activities conducted throughout the project. Delivery cadence pertains to the schedule and regularity of project deliverables. Projects may feature a single delivery, multiple deliveries, or periodic deliveries.

Single delivery refers to projects having one deliverable at the end of the project (for instance, a project whose goal is to re-engineer a process, where the delivery is not present until almost the end of the project during the rollout of the process). Multiple deliveries are projects with multiple components delivered at various times during the project (for example: a project to develop a new vaccine in which there are several phases of trials before the product is launched, or when some of the deliveries are developed separately instead of sequentially and all of the components are required to be finished before the project is considered complete). Periodic deliveries resemble multiple deliveries but follow a set schedule, such as monthly or bimonthly. We see periodic deliveries when developing a software application that may have internal deliveries biweekly, which are then periodically launched to the market.

The development approach method is determined by the project deliverable(s) type. The development method and type of deliverable(s) influence the number and cadence of project deliveries, selecting the project life cycle and its phases. Here are a few of the most common development methods:

- Predictive Project Life Cycle Model—Traditional Waterfall

The waterfall method represents a traditional or plan-driven approach to project management. It uses a waterfall, or sequential, life cycle where completing each phase before the next begins is a priority. The traditional life cycle—defining, planning, executing, and closing—is easily recognized in this method. The bulk of planning is done up front, leading to a single-pass execution of the project. This approach emphasizes the importance of project requirements and early planning to prevent mistakes during execution. Modifications to the project plan are discouraged and necessitate formal approvals. The waterfall method is a traditional practice that found its best use in software and hardware development. In this method, the project work flows downward like water falling over a ledge. The initial programs are designed by software developers and then handed over to programmers

to create the computer code. The programmers then hand the completed code to the quality control analysts who then test the code and turn it over to the customer for acceptance testing. This process flows logically and in a linear fashion, but if any problems are found, returning the work back to the previous step is very expensive—much like the difficulty of swimming back up a waterfall.

The waterfall method's stages are design, coding, testing, and user acceptance. In this method, the project work flows downward like water falling over a ledge. Software developers design the

initial program and then hand it over to programmers to create the computer code. The programmers hand the completed code to the quality control analysts who then test the code and send it to the customer for acceptance testing.

## Iterative Project Life Cycle Method

In the prior example, it was assumed that the process to complete the project can be clearly planned in advance. This is not always the case, since some projects require initial planning to begin executing the project but will be planned further after the team has more experience and knowledge. Consider the case of a company that wants to install a comprehensive computer system to replace their old individual systems that separately managed inventory, customer records, accounts receivable, accounts payable, general accounting, tax accounting, and human resources. These comprehensive computer systems are called enterprise resource planning (ERP) systems and became popular in the 1990s. The problem with planning this type of project is that there are too many unknown issues. To address this situation, the overall project is broken down into phases.

The different aspects of an ERP (enterprise resource planning) system are shown. These include inventory, services, human resources, MRP (material requirements planning), production, purchasing, CRM (customer relationship management), sales, and financials.

Phases in this context are different from the phases of the project life cycle described earlier. In this case, each phase is a full project with a deliverable.

There are three phases in an ERP project. Phase 1 is introduced as a project to document all the existing business processes and computer systems. In Phase 2, the software is identified and the contract is made. In Phase 3, the software is implemented. Since the entire company’s computer systems are being replaced, it is not possible up front to develop a reasonable timeline or total cost estimate. Instead, Phase 1 is introduced as a project to document all the existing business processes and computer systems. The deliverable for Phase 1 is a complete document that would provide an understanding of the magnitude of the ERP system that will be needed. What is unique about this type of iterative approach is that knowledge is built, and a specific deliverable comes from the phase; however, at the conclusion of the first phase, the decision is made whether Phase 2 will occur. This decision point is called a phase gate, and it is the opportunity for company leadership to decide if moving forward is prudent. If the outcome of Phase 1 shows that the company is not prepared for a major computer system upgrade or that the system is larger than they can likely afford, the project ends there before any more resources are expended.

## Benefits of Iterative Processes

The use of phases to accomplish an overall project outcome is one way of addressing uncertainty. In the ERP example, the company benefited from repeating the entire project life cycle all the way through to closing three times. In each iteration, the company learned more and refined the scope of the ERP system. Based on the outcomes of each phase, the company leadership was able to make better decisions about whether to move forward toward full implementation. This iterative process provided phase gates—or go/no go—where the overall project could be easily stopped before any more resources were expended. An additional benefit was that the internal users of the system learned and further communicated about how their business processes fit with the new systems so that by the time the systems requirements document went to the potential bidders, it was a tailored and more comprehensive view of the desired functionality. Before Phase 1, the system users did not even know what to ask for in a new

system. The iterative process allowed the organization to learn and set realistic expectations of the new system.

## Adaptive Life Cycle Model—Agile

## Agile

The agile approach uses a more adaptive or change-driven approach. It combines the predictive and interactive approach with the goal of refining the work and frequency of small deliverables. With this method, the planning and executing stage has multiple iterations and repeat frequently to obtain interim results before the project is completed. With agile projects, the project manager focuses on providing value to customers quickly, maintaining flexibility to meet changing business needs, and adding new requirements identified by stakeholders rather than quickly finalizing scope definition.
