# S2 2.5 A Project's Triple Constraint

## Learning Objectives

Define the project term triple constraint.

## A Project's Triple Constraint

An example of how triple constraint impacts a real-world project may help explain the concept. But before looking at an example of the project triple constraint, you will review some of the concepts of the triple constraint with a short video clip. The video shows how you can prioritize dimensions of the triple constraint to achieve a specific outcome. Keep these in mind for the following sections.

Watch "The Triple Constraint" (3:47) from LinkedIn Learning.

Contemporary wooden single-family house on river surrounded by oak forest. Sergey Makarov/iStock / Getty Images Plus via Getty Images.

You have worked hard and saved some money. You also have a very good credit rating. You decide that you would like to build a second home that you can use for vacations and weekend getaways. You have decided on a location. There are a number of decisions that you need to make, including the following:

- When would you like this vacation home completed and ready? Schedule
- How much money can you spend? Cost
- What features and amenities would you like to have? Scope/Quality

You decide to first look at features and amenities—the scope—and you come up with a wish list. This includes, as a start, the following:

- a master suite for you and your spouse
- four additional bedrooms, for children, in-laws, and friends

separate bathrooms for each bedroom a great room with a large wood-burning fireplace a separate woodshed gas heat since this will be a year-round vacation house air conditioning since this will be a year-round vacation house wood floors in all rooms except the kitchen redwood or cedar siding and roof shingles tiled floor in the kitchen an attached three-car garage a boathouse since it is on a lake, and you desire time on the water a shed or garage for snowmobiles a secure room for storage of hunting supplies an asphalt access road from the main road furnishings for the house It is now close to the end of winter, and you are thinking of getting started so you can have your cabin ready before next fall. You think six months should be plenty of time. Before continuing, stop for a minute and consider your dream vacation home. You have your wish list of features and amenities—the project scope. You know how much money you have available in the form of cash and what you are willing and able to borrow. You have decided on when you would like this project to be completed. These are your triple constraints. Now look at some things that could impact your triple constraint. You need running water in this house. The county was supposed to run a new water main that you would connect to. The county has postponed this new water main for one year. Your cost estimate was based on this connection. You could wait a year to build, affecting the schedule, or you could use water from the lake, which would require water treatment and purification capability, impacting cost, and county approval to siphon lake water, affecting both cost and schedule.

Environmental regulations change, and your planned asphalt road cannot be built as planned, affecting scope. You could change the type of asphalt, affecting cost. You could change to a graded dirt road, which would need regrading each year and affect maintenance cost. You could change to a gravel road, which may require patching each year and affect maintenance cost. Along with postponing the water main, the county has postponed installation of sewage lines for one year. Your cost estimate was based on this connection. You could wait a year to build, affecting schedule, or use some other means of waste disposal, affecting cost. Environmental regulations are quite strict on this. You are not permitted to run any waste into the lake. You could put in a septic system, affecting both cost and schedule. The winter turns out to be unexpectedly long and harsh. The snow has not melted, and the ground has not thawed as quickly as anticipated. You could delay the start, affecting the schedule. You could have an extra crew, so some work that was to be done in series can be done in parallel, affecting cost. You could delay some of the features, such as the boathouse and the shed or garage for snowmobiles, affecting both schedule and scope. After you have a cost estimate, but before you sign a contract, labor costs go up. Because the contract has not been signed, your costs increase. You can either postpone some of the construction, such as the boathouse, for a year, affecting both schedule and scope. You could just continue with the planned scope and schedule, affecting cost. The above are only a few hypothetical issues that could occur on your project. There are many more possibilities, as well as many other issues that may not be apparent until after your project begins. Construction contractors often include contract clauses to limit their risks due to unforeseen problems.
