# S1 2.4 Using Reports

## Learning Objectives

Describe the communication methods used by the project manager.

## Using Reports

Essential to the project’s success is communication. Remember that reports are a communications tool but will not replace all types of communications. Each stakeholder may have their own need for information beyond standard reporting measures. If reports are not easy to read and understand by the recipient, they are useless. The only reason to write a status report is to give and get pertinent information. This requires stakeholders to understand the report at a level that allows them to respond to a clear call to action. Know why you are writing the report before you spend the painstaking effort to prepare it. What do you need from the readers, and why should they meet your need? You must answer the question, "What is in it for me?" As you prepare and review reports, be sure they answer the crucial question, "Why should I care?" Reports can be designed to be given directly to stakeholders, posted on the project website, or discussed in project meetings. Closely linked with the status meeting is the selection of the report types a project will use. Good project reports communicate the current status of the project, including risks and issues the team is facing, and provide direction to the team for the immediate work period. They should also project the future performance of the project as known at this point. Reports should be a guide for communications and should be clear and concise regarding who is to do what and when tasks must be completed, in addition to detailing what has happened so far. The reports should keep a positive tenor without hiding or ignoring issues that must be addressed in order to keep the project on track. Success always breeds more success. Do not forget to celebrate successes during the course of the project. Project reports also provide a history of the project. This history can be very important in understanding the factors behind a project’s successes and failures and provides information that may be helpful downstream or on other similar projects. Reports delivered to customers who have contracted for services also represent an important source of evidence if there are disputes regarding project performance.

In many cases, organizational standards dictate key elements to which reports must adhere. Even with organizational requirements in place, a project manager should always work to ensure that all reports are clear, data driven, and aligned to the needs and roles of the audience. It is the project manager's responsibility to determine how to transmit key data to the different players in the project. To that end, he or she may use several different but related templates. You will walk through a meeting cycle and examine examples of some of these report templates.

## An example of a team weekly status meeting form

The weekly team call occurs on Friday before lunch. The team members are the leaders from each of the functional areas: design, marketing, manufacturing, and quality. Before the meeting, each leader does an update of their team’s status in an online tool. The project manager receives schedule updates on an ongoing basis, so she comes to this meeting with the goal of resolving the status of a small number of activities. During the meeting, each leader speaks to the status of their work: progress to plan, issues, and help they need from other portions of the team or management to resolve their issues. The project manager leads the discussion and ensures that she receives the information needed to update the schedule. She also leads the team through a discussion of issues and action items from the prior

week if they were not addressed by the team leads. Risks and the status of any mitigation activities are discussed. Based on the discussion, the project manager updates the team reports and assembles them into one document. For this team, the update document is published in an online tool where it can be retrieved, viewed, and printed by any team member.

In this example, the customer needs a weekly project status for roll-up within their organization. They have provided a one-page template that focuses on progress to contractually identified milestones until the project completes. The different template requires that the project manager perform a manual update of the report and the comparison of the baseline milestone dates with the actual or

projected dates. If a baseline date is projected to be missed, the customer expects to see this information as soon as possible, along with a description of the actions being taken to recover. Sometimes the call is canceled due to scheduling conflicts. Even if this happens, the report still has to be sent to the customer because it is a contractual requirement.

On a monthly basis, the management team reviews all the projects underway at the company. On average, 10 projects are reviewed over the course of a two-hour meeting. The management team expects each project manager to have updated their one-page project template in the online tool. The meeting is run by the PMO within the company that helped establish the reporting standards and format. In this example, the projects are listed by priority, and the project and budget statuses appear as either green, yellow, or red, in addition to the percent complete. Additionally, the team should discuss three highlights, three lowlights, and three issues. They should compare project milestones to the baseline from start to finish and discuss financial performance trends.

The monthly status report example is divided into three parts. The first part has information like project name, project manager name, project sponsor name, project owner name, reporting period, and estimated project end date. The second part is the project description in bullet points. The third part is a table with three columns. The first column is status. The second column is description, and the third column is the reason for deviation.

Once a month, the customer comes in for a set of formal and informal meetings. The formal meetings include a review of the project status that the project manager runs. Using the team’s prior weekly report and the customer meeting information from last month, the project manager covers project performance to schedule and financial performance for the project. Product and contract issues and discussions are also held at this time. The engineers from the customer will spend time with the project manager’s engineering team to do detailed reviews of specifications, tests, and quality data and proposed product changes made by both sides. Note: These are examples of meetings and reports but are by no means exhaustive. The project manager must be sure to do the following: Do what the organization requires. Give the complete picture of current progress in relation to the current plan. Identify and drive solutions to any and all issues. Set the stage for next steps and work to be performed. Celebrate the team’s successes. Be an advocate for the project, presenting the information in an honest and positive light. The project manager is a manager and project salesperson throughout this process. Superior communications skills will help ensure the probability of success.
