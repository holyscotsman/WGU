# S5 3.1 What Is Risk?

## Learning Objectives

Define risk and risk management.

What Is Risk?

Everything can be risky. Walking across the street is risky, although walking across a country road is less risky than a busy avenue in New York City. In project management, risk is defined as any uncertainty in an event or condition that may impact the project. It could impact scope, schedule, cost, or quality. You must evaluate all tasks and be aware of risk conditions, understand the likelihood or odds of their happening, and do what you can to avoid negative impacts. You generally think of risks as negative, but risk can create positive opportunities as well.

Risks come when uncertainty exists, which is generally always. It is your job to recognize that risks exist and manage them—avoid them altogether, mitigate them to reduce impact, transfer them to somewhere or someone else, or simply accept the risk and costs involved. According to PMBOK 7, the uncertainty performance domain addresses activities and functions associated with risk and uncertainty. Risks are bound to occur when uncertainty exists in defining requirements, and the presence of risk adds to the uncertainty. Further, ambiguity, volatility, and complexity are some of the factors that, if present, will increase risks associated with the project. The following are the performance domains as they relate to project risk and risk management. As you move through the lesson, you will see how and why these domains are integrated into the risk management process.

## Stakeholder Domain

The stakeholder analysis performed in the defining phase provides useful information about their interests, concerns, and needs. If not met, these elements could become risk factors for a project. A project team must work collaboratively with stakeholders to elicit their requirements and expectations, then attempt to resolve issues and concerns while developing the risk plan. Stakeholder interactions, interests, and influences vary throughout the project life cycle; if not monitored, these could manifest as risks. Further, periodic communication with the stakeholders is highly desirable, with a focus on new developments and changes in the risk management plan.

## Team Domain

Every team member must be involved in identifying risks by developing a comprehensive list of risks with associated impact and probability of occurrence.

## Development Approach Domain

Risk management will differ based on the development approach chosen. When requirements are identified with a greater level of confidence and with less uncertainty, a predictive development approach is employed. As you will see, the development of a risk register and risk management plan is often based on scope planning and the WBS. Cost and schedule risks can be minimized with a collaborative team effort in developing these plans. In an adaptive approach, uncertainty and ambiguity are often associated with defining requirements. Therefore, the risk associated with the project scope may be higher. Risk is minimized by iterative and incremental planning, wherein the scope of a sprint is minimized and focused on; a prioritized business value is considered a story and is executed in a sprint.

## Planning Domain

Project deliverables, organization requirements, market requirements, and legal or regulatory restrictions all contribute to project planning, and associated risks must be assessed. In a predictive planning approach, it is possible to develop a detailed WBS and forecast possible risks associated with each WBS work package.

## Delivery and Measurement Domains

Completion of requirements can be asserted and measured using acceptance criteria, technical performance measures, and the definition of done. However, if requirements are not complete or are not clear, then the delivery and measurement will be in jeopardy. If uncertainty in defining requirements is minimized, a predictive approach and consequent detailed development of scope will help in identifying, monitoring, and managing risks. If uncertainty and volatility are present in requirements, the scope is narrowed down using an adaptive approach so that acceptance criteria, measures, and the definition of done can be determined.

## PMBOK 7, 2021

Project risks or opportunities stem from the real world of uncertainty in the business, on the project, or from any associated action that can impact the outcomes of the project from internal or external influences. You can have uncertainty at the project level or at any activity or task within the project’s deliverables, resulting in risks. Organizations and stakeholders must be informed of risks in order to define what degree of risk is acceptable, and the project manager must understand the current culture’s attitude toward risk. According to the PMBOK, an organization’s risk acceptance can be classified into three levels: Risk appetite is the degree of uncertainty an entity is willing to take on in anticipation of a reward. Risk tolerance is the degree, amount, or volume of risk that an organization or individual will withstand. Risk threshold refers to measurements along the level of uncertainty or the level of impact at which a stakeholder may have a specific interest. Below that risk threshold, the organization will accept the risk. Above that risk threshold, the organization will not tolerate the risk (PMBOK, 2015). Each organization has its own practices, policies, and culture around risk, and this is a key conversation the project manager must have with the owner, sponsor, and stakeholders. Every project has risks, and the project manager and stakeholders are obligated to be aware of,

recognize, and manage risks proactively. Restaurants are full of risks in every aspect, including product (menu), quality of services, marketing to attract customers, competition, and cost management. For example, you establish your menu and pricing based on the cost structure you determine with your suppliers. You have advertised a farm-to-table policy, looking for local produce to meet the menu’s promises. There is a drought, and all the cilantro, a key herb in your Mexican cooking, gets destroyed. You check and find out you can get this herb imported from Mexico at a higher cost. This creates issues for you in your marketing and cost profile. You need the food to taste as promised so you buy the higher-priced food, absorb the cost, and provide a special featuring "Cilantro imported from Mexico!" When you set your budgets, you manage the risk by including a margin for food costs and spoilage. You know you need to accept the risk as part of doing business, and you plan for it in your cost planning. You understand that you need to have an "appetite" or threshold for this type of risk and a "tolerance" for it as a reality of doing business. This is an example of an operational issue. In the case of your project, you are concerned about the risks of meeting the project’s schedule, budget, or functions as expressed by your sponsor's or customer’s expectations.

When building out the restaurant as a project, there are many moving parts—for example, getting the electrical, plumbing, or decorations done to open the restaurant on schedule and on budget. The restaurant must be open by September to be ready for the high season. This is a key requirement. But what happens if not all of your suppliers complete their work on time? How would you manage that risk? In this case, there is no risk tolerance for the restaurant not opening on schedule. How do you manage the lack of risk tolerance for this schedule requirement? Since you cannot accept the risk, you must find a way to avoid it. One option would be to transfer the risk to the vendors with fixed price contracts and penalty clauses. This option will be discussed later in this section.
