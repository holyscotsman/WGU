# S5 2.3 Project Deliverables

## Learning Objectives

Prepare a work breakdown structure given a project scenario.

You have done your project definition and begun your project plan by defining the deliverables. The deliverables are concrete things you need to give to your customer in order for the project to be complete. In this restaurant scenario, you have defined your first project in the program to be creating your first restaurant. The deliverables are shown in the following chart.

The project is setting up a new restaurant. The first activity is project management. The key deliverables or activities that come under project management are procurement, systems, and procedures, menu, front of house, kitchen, and business plan.

As you review these deliverables, you see the titles for the concrete items you need to do. As stated before, you have two types of deliverables: Project management deliverables—the concrete items you need to manage this project. They are usually generic to a business or methodology adopted by a project manager for use to manage the team and create sound communications. Project-specific deliverables—in this case, your project-specific deliverables are required tasks for the following:

- procurement, systems and procedures
- menu
- front of the house
- kitchen
- business plan

In order to complete the business plan, which is a primary deliverable, you must create documents around each of the deliverables. You will do tasks to complete each of the deliverables and will set a time frame where the business plan is done last.

## Where Activity Information Goes

Once you have taken the deliverables and broken them into activities and tasks, you are in a position to validate your scope and begin assigning work. You take the activities and use them for individual assignments. Each task will be owned by someone. It is best practice that a task belongs to an individual. In this way, you are able to hold individuals accountable. You can never easily judge a team’s performance by having everyone responsible for everything. It also leads to confusion, conflict, and corruption. Some people will throw up their hands and not do any work. Others will get angry and try to do it all to avoid criticism. And in the end, you will not have a fully functioning team where you get the best value. Once you have all the activities and tasks defined, you can then use the activity and task information to do the following: Make assignments. Set priorities within activities across deliverables. Validate that the scope is fully planned. Feed into other project tasks like the budget and schedule. Inform the team, stakeholders, and customers of detailed planned activities, progress, and issues. Identify missing parts of the scope if you find that to be true. Examine changes in the change control approval process, comparing against planned activities to ensure they are not already being done or look for trade-offs to meet budget requirements or remind yourself why you were doing what you were doing and not what the change required. This helps you make informed decisions about change. Identify quality points for ensuring you meet quality requirements. Use the information as a framework for tracking and status reporting. Input information into project management software for control and communications. Create a resource matrix for describing skills required and tools needed to complete the project. It is critical that you use the WBS as a key planning tool, which becomes a management and communications tool. It lets you provide a pictorial presentation of the work to be done and helps validate the scope and what you are planning. The WBS will then help you plan for time and schedule and budget.
