# S7 1.1 Characteristics of Effective Teamwork

## Learning Objectives

Describe the characteristics of an effective project team.

## Characteristics of Effective Teamwork

You have probably heard the expression that TEAM stands for "Together Everyone Achieves More."

A chalkboard with TEAM, Together Everyone Achieves More written on it is shown. Nevenova/iStock / Getty Images Plus via Getty Images. It is such a common expression that it is often ignored. What exactly is a team? Often, in the workplace, a manager will gather a number of workers into a room and tell them, "You are now a team! Work together." Then the manager assigns a project. This is not a team. This is a group of people who were assembled with little planning or rational thought.

A team is a group of people working together to reach a mutually agreed-upon goal or objective. The team can be from many sources:

- line departments from the same company or organization
- project management organizations (PMO)
- external suppliers
- internal or external experts

specialized departments within the organization, such as technology, engineering, finance, manufacturing, marketing, or anywhere that the skills exist that the team requires. Often team members are people who may or may not know each other and have no experience working together. Implied in building a team is the need to bring the talent together that can achieve the organization’s goals on time to budget. They may be operational experts, but in this capacity, they are project- and not operations-oriented. They will have expertise that is required but may not know each other or have a history of working together. When you bring a team together, the project manager’s first challenge is to gain understanding of the shared goal, identify the roles and assignments, and build on soft people skills so that the team will learn to work together. This relies on interpersonal skills, communications, excellence, vision about the project, and responsiveness to issues and challenges. Everyone must have commitment to the team and the goals. In some organizations or cultures, the skills exist and it is a matter of directing the team forward. In all cultures, norms exist that must be built into team-building schedules and activities. This happens in any circumstance where a team is required and the goals are clear but the people are different. And no two people are alike. Examples of differences include the following:

- international or personal cultures

orientations (e.g., engineers often think very differently than accountants) experience levels company cultures, especially where external people are used with an internal project location of the resource—at the project center or anywhere in the world—and virtual talents

Have you ever watched an ant colony building a nest? While hiking through Mount Rainier's forests you may come across a large mound of tiny sticks and pine needles. These mounds are created by the western thatching ant (Formica obscuripes). Thatching ants scavenge plant material from all over the forest floor to build their nests, which can be up to eight feet (2.4 meters) tall. Thatching ants will often create their own "highways" radiating out from their mounds. The ants travel back and forth along the same paths so often that they wear down the moss, and any loose plant debris is gathered up and added to the nest or used for food. The omnivorous ants also scavenge for dead animals, collect any live animal small enough for them to overpower, and herd aphids for their nectar. They are the epitome of teamwork. Each ant knows what they need to do, with minimum direction. When a piece of building material is too big or heavy for a single ant, others immediately come to assist. No one ant, alone, could build a nest. Have you ever seen a group of geese migrating? They will often fly in a V formation or in a straight-line formation. Every few minutes the leader drops to the back of the formation, and a new bird takes its place. They instinctively work as a team and help each other. The formation they fly in creates a draft that makes it easier for the followers to fly, without expending as much energy as the leader. When the leader gets fatigued, it falls to the back of the formation to recover, and a new leader moves up. This allows the group to fly much further than any single bird could fly. And the "quacks" that you hear are the birds encouraging each other to fly. If one goose becomes too fatigued to carry on or becomes injured, it will separate from the group so the group is not held up. However, it is not left alone. At least one more member of the flock will accompany the fatigued or injured bird until it is well enough to continue on.

A group of migrating cranes following a leader. EBMarketa/iStock / Getty Images Plus via Getty Images. You might be thinking, "So what? Ants and birds do not think. This behavior is genetically programmed into them." Correct, but it can serve as an example of how much all creatures can accomplish when they cooperate and work together. These creatures exhibit some key characteristics of effective teams: There is uniform agreement on the end goal or objective. All team members contribute to reaching the end goal or objective. Team members cooperate and work together. If one team member has a problem with a task, others willingly assist. Team members each do what they are best at. Team members encourage each other’s efforts. Team members have a feeling of unity of purpose. The team functions better as a group than as individuals.

Take the example of an Amish barn raising. Much like ants, the entire community comes together to help build a barn for one of the locals. The teamwork is fantastic. Everyone knows what needs to be done. Everyone works toward the common, agreed-upon goal—helping a neighbor build a barn for their farm. No one seeks or expects individual recognition or reward. With this teamwork, an entire barn is constructed in a matter of days.

## Team Cohesiveness, Trust, and Motivation

How do you generate positive characteristics in project teams? There are three essential drivers that must be achieved:

- cohesiveness
- trust
- motivation

In a football match, one player is trying to stop another player from reaching the goalpost. danp68/iStock / Getty Images Plus via Getty Images. Anyone who has ever watched or participated in a team sport knows about cohesiveness, trust, and motivation. Look at an example in American football. The team acts as one, everyone on offense knows what the play will be, and everyone on defense is committed to stopping the play. The quarterback trusts the other players to block the other team so he can pass the ball. He also trusts the receiver to be where he is supposed to be to receive. And everyone is motivated to win. Sometimes the trust of the quarterback and the receiver results in success for the offense. Sometimes the defense does a better job of disrupting the play. Regardless, the team members need to be cohesive and trust each other to do their tasks on the next play.

As the project manager, your first priority is to bring your team together and foster a sense of shared responsibility to complete the project on schedule. In the case of the restaurant example, this will include the contractors and workers and the systems people, as well as the chef and staff, and the restaurant manager and workers. The team must create all the components of the restaurant according to the project plan. Plans are created by the team to gain clear direction. Often, unfortunately, plans are created but without team buy-in. Those plans are useless. There is very little that can be done for an organization without a team. When you put people together to build a team, you immediately have differences and things in common. To make the team work, you have to build the common ground and get the team to the stage of performance.
