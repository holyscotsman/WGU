# S4 1.6 Traditional Project Management Methods

## Learning Objectives

Describe traditional project management methods.

## Traditional Project Management Methods

Most of our discussions up to this point have focused on predictive or traditional project management methods. A defining characteristic of traditional methods is that the path from project beginning to project end is linear, and sequential steps are followed—define, plan, execute, and close. The steps are followed chronologically, and there is virtually no overlap. These traditional approaches are logical and work well in situations where the project outcomes can be clearly defined in advance and the project environment is stable. Most project organizations make use of traditional methods.

Traditional project management is a linear process consisting of four phases: define, plan, execute, and close.

## The Context for Selecting Traditional Project Management Methods

Some of the characteristics of a project environment well suited for traditional project management methods include the following:

a well-defined project outcome

a low-complexity project outcome

a project scope that is unlikely to change risks that are low and identifiable

projects that use known/stable technologies

projects that are not resource constrained

In short, traditional methods generally depend on being able to reliably plan the project in advance and then execute the plan with minimal changes. If the environment is turbulent, traditional methods will be inefficient in incorporating the large volume of changes during execution.

## Potential Problems When Using Traditional Project Management

## Methods

Companies can incur great expense during the planning of a project. If the benefit of the planning process is that the project can be executed smoothly and achieve the intended outcomes within the budget and schedule constraints, then the money was well spent. However, if during project execution, the project needs to be continually replanned in order to accommodate changes, the money spent planning was wasted.

An assessment of project volatility needs to be done in advance. Volatility is the level of instability in the project environment. Using a traditional project management method in a volatile environment can lead to the project not meeting its scope, budget, and/or cost requirements. The evaluation of volatility complements the earlier recognition of complexity and uncertainty that were characteristics of the project outcome or process used to create the outcome.

Even in a stable environment, the project manager must determine the appropriate level of planning required. Overplanning is a waste of resources, but underplanning does not provide the team with enough information to support executing the project. Knowing how much planning is sufficient can be very difficult and is an art form learned through experience and from lessons learned.
