# S5 4.2 Project Budgeting: Estimating Costs

## Learning Objectives

Explain how to construct a project budget.

## Cost Management and Project Objectives

The project budget will be the standard that the project costs are measured against. Cost management is the process of measuring how close actual costs are to the budget and then making changes in project execution as needed. Recall that time, cost, and scope are the three triple constraints, so managing the cost of the project will likely be very important to some of the stakeholders.

The PMBOK 7 guide details the performance domains in relation to the budget as the Planning, Measurement, and Uncertainty domains.

## Planning Domain

Budgeting may be performed mostly up front on predictive projects or in an iterative or incremental approach on adaptive projects. Planning includes creating estimates for work effort and costs associated with people and physical resources.

## Measurement Domain

Timely and accurate cost information enables the project team and stakeholders to make good decisions. Measurements are valuable not so much for their own sake but for the conversations and decisions they enable. A well-planned budget is used as an indicator that can predict changes or trends and may help to keep the project from exceeding its budget or an indicator reflecting past performance.

## Uncertainty Domain

Project teams should consider economic factors such as volatility in prices, availability of resources, ability to borrow funds, and inflation and deflation as they construct their budget. Some approaches to dealing with uncertainty are to prepare for multiple outcomes or build resilience to potential sudden changes, and these may require extra funds (contingency or management reserve) in the budget.

The project budget is an important measure of project performance. When the planning process is complete, the project manager will take a snapshot of the project budget. This snapshot is called the baseline budget. Baselining means freezing the planned budget so that actual costs can be compared to it throughout the remainder of the project. The baseline budget is the approved budget and will be used as the standard for comparison of actual costs throughout the life of the project and identifying variances.

That is not to say that the budget cannot change during project execution. If changes to time, cost, or scope are identified during execution, they must be approved by the customer and the project leadership generally through a change control process. The cost management process will still report cost overruns and cost savings compared to the baseline budget; however, differences can be documented and explained by the approved changes.

## Elements of a Project Budget

The two critical elements of a budget are the amount of expenditure and when the expenditure is expected to occur. The amount will include the direct costs, indirect costs, and any general and administrative overhead costs.

Direct costs are costs that are directly attributable to completing the project work. The direct costs can be attributed to specific work activities identified in the WBS. They often include labor, materials, supplies, and equipment.

Direct overhead costs (also known as indirect costs) are costs from the project that are shared across the work activities. If workspace, supervision, project administration, equipment, or other resources are not related to a specific work activity, they are included as overhead and will be allocated to all work activities. These are also called indirect costs because they cannot be directly attributed to a specific project work activity.

General and administrative costs (G&A) are overhead costs from the project organization. These are outside of the project team but often include expenses from corporate services such as legal, human resources, and payroll. These support functions are provided by the project hosting organization but cannot be related to a specific project work activity, so they will be allocated to all projects and project work activities. These are also called "corporate overheads" because they are not specifically related to one project but are shared or born by all activities of the business.

## The Purpose of Estimating Project Costs

The budget represents a commitment to the customer of what their project will cost. It goes hand-in-hand with the team’s commitment to the schedule and the scope. The project team is committing to the customer what they will deliver, when they will deliver it, and for how much money.

Internally, the budget serves as a guide to the project team as they execute the work. Throughout the project, the actual expenditures will be compared to the budget to identify any situations where adjustments may be needed. If the actual costs are higher than the budget because the planning was not accurate, then the project manager will need to decide if replanning is required. If these costs are legitimate, the project organization will need to determine if the customer will pay the additional cost or if the costs will be absorbed by the project organization. This is often a major point of discussion when risks are reviewed. Who will pay for the impact of the risk is built into the budget.

The monitoring of the actual cost and the budgeted costs also helps the project manager identify situations where the project team may need more training or personnel with different skills to complete the work as planned. A person who has less skill than expected in the plan could cost less but might take more time. This needs to be addressed if it impacts the budget.

Project Budget – Actual Expenses = Budget Savings (or budget shortfall)

Monitoring and controlling can be equated to checking budget performance and making adjustments as needed. Monitoring will occur throughout the life of the project so that changes can be made along the way rather than arriving at project completion only to find out that the budget has been significantly overspent.

## Top-Down and Bottom-Up Estimating Methods

The top-down method presented earlier serves two purposes. First, top-down estimates support the project during the definition phase. Someone with experience with similar projects can provide an estimate of the overall project cost. This estimate can be used to develop the project proposal and support the project proposal evaluation process. This top-down estimate is only a rough estimate, but it should have sufficient accuracy to support the defining phase. It is key to document all the assumptions made during the process to help understand variances if they occur.

Once the project is accepted, it moves to the planning phase. The top-down budget can still serve as a guide by using the ratio or allocation methods to allocate the overall budget estimate into subcategories. At this point, the functional experts who will be responsible for completing the work must determine whether the allocated budget is sufficient to complete the required work. Functional experts evaluating the resources required to complete the specific work identified in the WBS is considered a bottom-up method. The top-down and bottom-up then meet at a negotiated middle ground where the project has sufficient resources to complete the work and the customer is satisfied with the overall budget estimate for the project.
