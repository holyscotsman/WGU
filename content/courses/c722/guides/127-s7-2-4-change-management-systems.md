# S7 2.4 Change Management Systems

## Learning Objectives

Describe project change management processes.

## Change Management

Change management is a key operation for the project manager. All the new information and ideas learned by the team and stakeholders need to be evaluated and resolved. Resolution can be changed to the current project, held for future projects, set aside, or any other action the team recommends and the stakeholders approve. To do this analysis, the evaluation of the triple constraint is the best guidance. Does the schedule, functions, or budget change? Will the quality of the project be put at risk? Can you afford to do it? Does the project meet the client's needs without it now, and will it have future value? How critical is the change in the operation for meeting the business strategy? All these questions require answers before the work, function, or cost is added to the project. Organizations often use software to manage project changes. These change control systems are part of the larger software program that manages all project information. The change control system often includes forms to support documenting proposed changes, reports to track change proposals, and workflow management processes to route changes for review and approval. Regardless of whether a computer-based or a manual system is used, a process must be in place to manage change. Change is inevitable during projects, so managing it efficiently is important. Proposed changes that are not acted upon as identified can often become more costly to implement later in the project or in time of rework of the project’s functional delivery. The change management system is in place to formally identify, evaluate, decide, and communicate project changes.

Evaluating change means ensuring that the appropriate stakeholders provide input to identify the costs and benefits of the change. When sufficient information is gathered, project leadership and the customer must decide if the proposed change will be implemented. Communicating change involves notifying the project team of the change, updating the project plans to incorporate the change, and implementing the change. If the proposed change is not approved, then it still needs to be documented and communicated so that the team is aware of its status. Once the change has been evaluated and a decision has been made, the plans need to be revised to incorporate the change. The work breakdown structure (WBS), budgets, and schedules all can be impacted, and resources must be committed to complete the change. The project manager must now manage the delivery of the change as part of the project and report on completion along with the rest of the project’s work. The ultimate project performance measurements will include the change.

## Structured Change Management Phases

A structured change management approach, consisting of four essential phases—prepare for the change, plan, manage, and reinforce and sustain the change—is very important in supporting scope change. This approach, linked with the PMBOK process groups, is key to the success of change within projects. Structured Change Management Phases 1. Initiation—Prepare for change Obtain necessary resources and documentation to organize the processes of change. 2. Planning This is the integration of formal plans and change management plans into the overall change management and project management plan. 3. Management This is the management of implementation of the change based on the defined change management plan. 4. Reinforcing and Sustaining the Change The final component of the change process is a thorough evaluation of the sustainability of a change. In some instances, changes have a lasting impact on work activity performance, making the change both permanent and sustainable. This long-term evaluation is a critical step in the change control process.

## Controlling

During project execution, the project manager and team must control the use of all resources, including time, money, and other goods and services. They must use these resources according to company policy and the project plan's commitments. There is a need to balance detail with action and information. The project baseline is a contract between the team and the stakeholders. It must be monitored to ensure that commitments are followed through on. This is accomplished by having a vigilant policy of controlling what is done and which resources are used. The use of resources should comply with the commitments made in the plan. Project controls are the data gathering, management, and analytical processes used to predict, understand, and constructively influence the time and cost outcomes of a project or program. These controls are exercised through the communication of information in formats that assist effective management and decision-making.
