# S3 1.8 Net Present Value (NPV)

## Learning Objectives

Describe how financial concepts are used for project selection purposes.

Net present value documents on a table. designer491/iStock / Getty Images Plus via Getty Images.

Net present value (NPV) Net present value (NPV) is a financial measure of the total future benefits of a project minus the costs of the project. If the future benefits (revenue, income, savings) are greater than the costs of the project, NPV is positive. If the costs of the project outweigh the future benefits, NPV will be negative. So overall, negative NPV is bad, positive NPV is good, and projects with higher NPV have greater benefits to companies. Earlier you learned about the time value of money, which said that money received in the future is not as valuable as money received now. NPV takes into consideration the time value of money by discounting future benefits. Although this makes the gathering of data and the calculation more complicated, the result is a very realistic estimate of a project’s value to a company. NPV uses the concept of future net cash flows, which means that to evaluate a project, you need to know the costs (cash outflows) and benefits (cash inflows) for the entire working life of the project outcome. If a company is evaluating a project to replace a current packaging machine, it will need to estimate the cost of installing the machine (the project cost), the cost of maintaining the machine, and the benefits (operational savings derived) for its operating life.

## Table: NPV for a packaging machine project

The table shows the NPV evaluation for the packaging machine project. The company has identified that the project to install the machine will cost $350,000 (Year 0). Additional annual maintenance costs of $2,500 occur across the 10-year life of the machine. In Year 5, the machine will require a major overhaul, which is reflected in the outflow of $7,500. These costs are all identified as cash outflows in their appropriate years. Installing the machine will bring the company efficiencies in the form of labor and material cost savings of $75,000 per year, which are shown as cash inflows (savings) in each year of the machine’s 10-year life. Depending on the situation, a company may identify cash inflows based on any combination of revenue, savings, or other benefits. In this example, the company specifically experiences a labor cost savings, which they account for as a cash inflow each year. The net cash flows for all 11 years (the initial project Year 0 and the 10 years of machine use) are then discounted based on an interest rate. Companies often identify the interest rate based on their cost of borrowing money or their required rate of return on investment, but either way, the rate is determined by the company.

NPV can be calculated using a spreadsheet program, such as Microsoft Excel’s NPV function, or by hand using a financial calculator. The net present value of the packaging machine project is $11,375. That means that over the life of the installed machine, it will positively affect the cash flow of the company. This meets the criteria that positive NPV is good, and now this project can be compared to the NPV of other projects to determine if it is the best use of company funds. Notice the effect of accounting for the time value of money using discounting. In Year 10, the net cash flow is $72,500, but when the discount rate (15%) is taken into consideration, the discounted net cash flow for Year 10 is only $17,921, which represents a substantial reduction in the value of the future cash flow. Another way to look at this is that to have $72,500 in 10 years, you would need to invest $17,921 at 15% today. Now you will use NPV to evaluate the construction delivery truck example described earlier. Recall that the construction materials company is replacing a truck-tractor that is used to deliver materials to customer worksites. A crane with the new automated uncoupling feature would cost an additional $10,000 but would create labor savings of $22,000 annually because a helper would not need to accompany the driver on each delivery. The payback period analysis shows that the annual labor savings of $22,000 would recoup the extra $10,000 of purchasing costs in just 5.45 months. Although this information is useful, the analysis does not take into consideration that the company will save the $22,000 in labor costs for the entire life of the truck-tractor, nor does it consider that there is a time value of money. NPV analysis would include the initial additional cost of $10,000, the annual labor savings of $22,000 over the 10-year life of the vehicle, and it would discount the future savings in order to account for the time value of money.

## Table: NPV analysis of a truck-tractor purchase

Notice in the table that in the outflows, only the additional $10,000 of purchasing cost is included. Because all other costs are the same (maintenance, training, purchase), the company is using NPV to just compare the additional cost and the additional savings of selecting the crane with the automated decoupling device. Subsequently, the inflows only show the annual labor savings of $22,000. Notice that discounted net cash flow shows that the savings have less value to the company in later years. That is the effect of the time value of money based on the discounting rate of 18%. The discount rate is prescribed by the company based on their cost of capital or internal standards. The NPV analysis reflects the initial additional cost of $10,000 and will result in a positive benefit of $88,870 over the life of the vehicle. The short payback period of 5.45 months and the large savings to the company ($88,870) would make a compelling case for moving forward with this project.
