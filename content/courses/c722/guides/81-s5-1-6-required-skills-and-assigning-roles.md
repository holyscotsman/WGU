# S5 1.6 Required Skills and Assigning Roles

## Learning Objectives

Describe common tools used to assign project team roles.

## Project Manager and Team Project Skills

The skills required by the project team are sometimes referred to as knowledge, skills, and abilities (KSAs). If the team does not have the necessary KSAs, the project will likely not be successful. First, the project requirements and deliverables must be identified. From this, the skills required to execute the project, fulfill the requirements, and meet the deliverables can be determined.

The first set of skills and abilities, and possibly the most important, are a particular set of project manager skills. These are the skills needed to effectively deal with people on a daily basis. Sometimes known as leadership skills, people skills, or soft skills, these are especially critical for the project manager in functional and matrix organizations.

Although economists have typically focused on cognitive skills, such as intelligence, literacy, and mathematical abilities in analyzing employment trends, recently they have started to look at noncognitive "soft" skills like self-esteem, motivation, and sympathy. Although economists have developed a lot of evidence showing the impact of cognitive skills on labor market outcomes, wages, occupation, and employment, they did not really know the importance of these socioemotional skills.

New evidence shows that these soft skills are both as important as cognitive skills in determining labor market outcomes and are affecting human behavior in general.

## Key Noncognitive Skills for a Project Manager

## Leadership

Ask 100 people what leadership is, and you will likely get 101, or more, definitions. Here are just a few:

"A leader is one who knows the way, goes the way, and shows the way." —John C. Maxwell

"Leadership is the art of influencing human behavior through ability to directly influence people and direct them toward a specific goal." —Gen. Omar N. Bradley, Chief of Staff

"[Leadership is] the process of influencing people by providing purpose, direction, and motivation while operating to accomplish the mission and improve the organization.” —Field Manual (FM) 6-22, Army Leadership

The common thread in these definitions would seem to be that a leader influences the actions and behaviors of others. Notice that these definitions do not make mention of any management position or title.

## Teamwork

A team is any group of people brought together and organized to work cooperatively to reach a commonly agreed upon and understood goal or objective. Note several keywords: organized, agreed upon, and understood. It is up to the leader, in this case the project manager, to make sure the group meets the three key items.

## Communication

Communication is much more than one person speaking to another. It is a two-way street. One person sends a message; a second person receives the message and responds. And each must ensure that the other understands the message sent. The message may be verbal or written, but it can also come through body language as well. It is important not to speak jargon or acronyms that others may not understand.

## Negotiation and Conflict Management

Conflict management and negotiation can sometimes feel like butting heads. This should and can be avoided. Conflict occurs when two or more parties do not agree. Remember, not all conflict is bad. Constructive conflict takes place when individuals come together to redefine or strengthen their relationship for the greater good of the parties involved. This can lead to a more beneficial arrangement or an improved product or service. Negotiation is the art of two or more parties reaching an agreement on their differences. The agreement should occur without litigation or physical conflict. Integrative bargaining is the preferred negotiation and conflict management path.

## Self-Control

In brief: Do not lose your temper. People tend to lose respect for others who do not control themselves in the workplace. Your leadership will suffer if you lose control. If you cannot control yourself, you will not be able to control situations that arise in the course of every project.

## Flexibility and Creativity

Projects are unique undertakings, so they have unique challenges. As you have seen, things change during the course of the project life cycle, and knowledge grows beyond what you knew when you planned the project. Therefore, you must remain flexible. Recall the example of building a vacation and retirement home in an earlier lesson. What happened in that scenario?

Things changed.

You had to be flexible to address these changes.

You needed some creativity to overcome some of the issues involved.

A unique undertaking indicates that some creativity will be required to accomplish the necessary tasks.

## Professionalism

Professionalism is the way you act, the way you dress, and the image you project. It is the perception people have about you, based on observation of your appearance and behaviors, and is often defined or influenced by the profession or culture. A perception of being a professional can produce a respectful attitude toward you and your work from those around you. It can also induce others to be more prone to follow your direction and your example.

## Ethics

Ethical and moral behaviors are often defined by one’s culture. Some people define being ethical as doing the right thing even when nobody's looking. Ethics goes beyond having an ethics manual. It means understanding and following ethical and moral behaviors. The project manager, as the project leader, sets the example. Remember, Enron Corporation had a 65-page ethics manual that was ignored by senior management.

## Decisiveness

There are times when a decision must be made, even if you may not have all the data. This is decisiveness. A lack of decisiveness can frustrate and demotivate the project team, in addition to the time and money wasted while the project manager procrastinates. Avoiding a decision is really a decision to stay with the status quo or do nothing.
