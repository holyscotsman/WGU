# Section 2: The Project Life Cycle Summary

## Section 2: The Project Life Cycle Summary

This section used a big-picture view of the project life cycle to map out the activities that occur throughout the project. It also addressed the triple constraint of scope, budget, and schedule. This section pointed out that the project life cycle is a core concept in PMBOK 7 that provides a structured way to manage a project from conception to completion and acts as a framework for managing the project throughout its entire duration. The key phases are defining, planning, execution, and closure. You also learned the project life cycle is integrated into the Development Approach and Life Cycle performance domain, emphasizing the importance of aligning its development approach with its life cycle phases to deliver business value throughout the project. Models, methods, and artifacts, or the tools and tailoring are used to assist with developing and moving through the phases of the project life cycle. Using the define, plan, execute, close model of project phases, you saw the typical activities that occur in each phase. During the defining phase, the project proposal is developed to support the project selection process. The defining phase ends with the decision to accept or reject the project proposal and the subsequent approval of the project charter. The planning phase is when project requirements are gathered from the stakeholders and the project team identifies the work activities necessary to produce the project deliverables. The planning phase ends with the approval of the project scope, budget, and schedule. The executing phase is when the team uses the plan to produce the project outcomes. This

When monitoring processes detect that the project deliverables are completed, the closing phase begins. During the closing phase, all procurement activity is completed, the customer accepts delivery of the finished project, and project documentation is finished. Lessons learned are written during the closing phase. The "lessons learned" document details what went well and identifies what could be improved. These lessons learned help the project organization improve how projects are run and will be a source of information for future project managers. The section wrapped up by explaining the triple constraints of scope, budget, and schedule. Scope is the constraint that sets the standard for the functionality of the finished project. The budget is the targeted cost of the finished project. The schedule is the targeted completion date. The project manager must balance the trade-offs between all three, realizing that changes to one will require adjustments to at least one of the other two. If the customers want the project delivered a week earlier, for example, then they must be willing to reduce the scope or increase the budget accordingly. In everything discussed, it is critical for the project manager to communicate with all the stakeholders and team members and to act as the pivotal person in identifying and resolving issues as they occur.
