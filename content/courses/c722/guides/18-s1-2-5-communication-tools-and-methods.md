# S1 2.5 Communication Tools and Methods

## Learning Objectives

Describe the communication methods used by the project manager.

## Example Communication Tools

Project teams now have a large number of tools at their disposal to help the team collaborate and to help the project manager with the management of the project. The tools can be both commercial and open source. They fall into several broad categories, as follows:

## Scheduling Tools

These are fundamental tools of the project manager because they are how the schedule commitments of the team are captured and tracked. These tools and the data contained in them are a primary source of data that the project manager uses to communicate. Whether using a Gantt chart to drive discussion with the team or filtering a set of milestones for the executive discussion, every project manager needs to have a scheduling tool and keep it updated. Scheduling tools range from stand-alone copies of Microsoft Project or an open-source equivalent, such as ProjectLibre, to large enterprise-wide tools that allow for roll-up of project schedules and resources across the enterprise. Many of the enterprise tools also include integrated reporting, risk, issue, change control, program, and portfolio management options.

## Collaboration Tools

These started with the first electronic repositories where reports could be archived. Initially, they provided nothing more than an electronic file cabinet, but these tools have seen an explosion in their functionality and now include the ability to perform status reporting, task assignment, issue management, and threaded team discussions. By integrating the functionality from several tools into one, the goal is to improve team performance by providing a more complete team experience. An

example would be when a team using several tools—such as email, instant messaging, a schedule tool, an issue tracking tool, and a threaded discussion tool—converts to a cloud-based collaboration tool that provides all of this functionality in one place. Often the organization or project manager will choose a software product to help manage the creating and communication of the plan and tasks to be performed. This is an easier way to produce some charts for the team to review. A common program that is used is Microsoft Project.

## Communication Methods In Virtual Teams

A virtual team can be defined as a team whose members are not physically colocated on a regular basis during the project. That means that the team members could be in different parts of the city, in different states, or in different countries. Studies have shown that the frequency and quality of communications drops off rapidly with distance and lack of physical contact. Differences in time zones and language will further exacerbate these problems. So how does a project manager communicate when faced with a virtual team? The project manager must plan for success, and it is more challenging to ensure the success of virtual teams. The basic tenets of project management still apply. Collaboration tools offer a project manager a great foundation to ensure that the team has the ability to work together in real time. Shared threaded discussions are superior to point-to-point instant messaging because the contents are available for all team members to access and contribute to. Using tools like Google Drive lets collaboration happen on critical reports, enables data collection that you can convert into actionable information, and supports general idea sharing.

Regular conference calls and online meetings help to clarify information and create the human interaction needed to create a team. Software is available to facilitate online meetings with voice and information sharing (e.g., Skype, Zoom, Microsoft Teams, GoTo Meeting, Webex). The core to all of this is understanding everyone’s experience, ideas, and contributions. Always remember that you are dealing with people and that the needs of those people differ, but the project’s goals are specific and shared.

Using work breakdown structures (WBS) ensures that everyone understands what has to be delivered and who is responsible for doing what and when. The WBS will be discussed later, but it is a key tool so that people understand what is expected of them and how they will be held accountable. It is vital that everyone on the team understands what role they have and what is expected. This is especially important in virtual teams. People get into the most trouble when they do not feel connected or do not understand what they need to do to meet the team’s needs. The 8 and 80 rule is an ideal guide for a project manager to aid in communications. It states that a project manager should avoid controlling tasks of less than eight hours or team members will feel micromanaged. As a project manager, you must find the line between hovering over people and ignoring them. This is where the "80" part of the rule comes in. The project manager should not let more than 80 hours (or two weeks) go by without checking in. This ensures that team members do not feel isolated, do get timely feedback, and are aware of any changes that may affect their specific tasks. It also keeps problems or issues from growing too big. With all of this said, nothing replaces the human contact needed to build relationships. Writing is helpful but can be isolating and leave individuals unable to connect. Suggestions for curing this include the following: Have virtual meetings using video conferencing tools. Skype or Hangouts from Google are easy to use. GoTo Meeting is a great tool for collaboration and allows participants to share their desktops. There are many products out there, and often organizations have their own standards. Honor the differences that exist. Hold meetings at different times to accommodate different time zones. Set a universal language and make translation available as necessary. Do not ignore the differences. Recognize them and the human needs they create.

Assign leadership for meetings to different people to allow for inclusion and a sense of belonging. Always give people an opportunity to ask questions and speak about needs and opinions. Review the cultural differences if you are dealing with people from other cultures. Often people have different norms for speaking about issues. For example, there are cultures where saying no feels impolite, so they may not tell you when they have an issue with what you are asking them to do. You need to find ways to build bridges among the multiple cultures relevant to your team. Always say thank you.

## Project Manager Conflict Communication

## Skills

One of the greatest challenges for the project manager is communication. It is said that 80% of all problems come from poor communication. It is critical to communicate clearly, often, and with sensitivity to the project’s needs, the team’s characteristics, and the individuals' strengths. Good project managers need to direct, provide tools to, and get out of the way of the people on the project, but they also need to be sure to come back often enough to ensure that issues are identified and resolved. Building teams of people with different backgrounds and skills creates new outcomes that further the organization toward the goals it has set. You would expect there to be different perspectives and opinions and should not be shocked if conflict occurs. Conflict is real and to be expected and even often desired. Conflict is created by combining people of different skills and backgrounds; setting aggressive deliverables and schedules; asking people to keep their heads down and work on the results needed, sometimes without seeing the whole picture; and living in a different reality than people are accustomed to and going through the normal phases of human relationships—dating, honeymoon, reality (seeing each other’s warts), and crunch time are all stages that happen to people on projects. When you do this, you are doing your job, but you need to understand that this will cause conflict. So what do you do now?

Unresolved conflict causes morale problems and loss of productivity and often loss of critical talent. You will also see emotional reactions that confuse the individuals and the team. A project manager needs to recognize conflict and channel it quickly, establishing a healthy work environment for everyone. To manage conflict, go back to the basics of communications: Communicate clearly and often, and if you think you have done enough, do more! Communicate over and over again. Follow these basic communication tenets: Establish expectations for the team on what they are to do, how they should communicate with each other, and what to do if there are issues. The communications plan sets the path to that. Make it clear that issues are to be raised and where and when to do so. You will always have issues. Address conflict immediately. Be certain everyone understands you see your role as a leader and as a mediator. Make it safe for people to come to you with an issue. Be certain not to take sides but to listen and hear the issues and stay on the professional side of the issue. Do not address individual personal flaws or take a side, but speak to the work that has to be done. You cannot fix people, but you can fix business problems. Stay focused on the project and its goals. Your job is to help people get the work done so that the project will meet its goals, stay on budget, and stay on time according to the agreedto functionality. It is critical not to take on more than the project is defined to do. People must stay focused and have a clear understanding of their responsibilities. Remember you are dealing with a team and individuals, and they are motivated by what they want. Be certain to understand the WIIFM (“What's in it for me?”) for each person and to verify that you want to help them achieve their goals on the project, and discuss how their goals align with the project. If you cannot do that, you may have a mismatch on the team. Conflict is an opportunity! By addressing conflicts and objections, you may learn something about the project that is critical. The sooner you understand the issue, the better chance you have to fix it before it grows into a disaster. Small issues are much better than major problems that come late in the game. As hard as you may try, you never have a perfect project plan. You will have issues, and understanding them early will keep the project on its critical path.
