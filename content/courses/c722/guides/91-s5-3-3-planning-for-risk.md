# S5 3.3 Planning for Risk

## Learning Objectives

Describe how to construct a risk register.

## Risk Planning

Risk planning is the process of reviewing every aspect of the project to identify what risks may occur. You can do this by examining internal and external influences. You want to create a risk management plan that considers the following: You should know the methods and procedures you will follow to evaluate risks, including reviews, tools, sources of information, and means to understand what risks you are potentially facing. Plan for people resource management, or who will do what and when. This will ensure that you have proper leadership roles and responsibilities for managing risks and the team is aware of what they might have to do. Outline the timing and time constraints to understand when each risk may occur. Risks occur at particular times in the project. You need to be prepared for the possibility, and then, after you are past that time in the project, update your risk monitoring to show that the risk is no longer a threat. Funding for risk management and resolution is a key part of the budgeting process.

## Risks Associated with Projects

As has been said, by definition, a risk is anything that can impact the outcomes of the project either positively or negatively. You have reviewed your planned deliverables, activities, and tasks to see what can go wrong. You need to review these based on influences on your actions. These come in many flavors, and you can use the following categories to help you identify risks: Organization—resources, funding, priorities, competition with other projects, and reliance or dependencies on other actions in the organization

External influences—partners or contractors, regulation or government, competitive market influences, customer expectations and availability, environmental factors such as weather, etc. Technical—technology availability and cost, complexity of the technical requirements, performance and reliability, quality, project requirements, costs, etc. Review every task identified in the WBS and decide what can go wrong. You do this within the context of the whole project, and this process enhances your entire planning process. One tool recommended in PMBOK is a risk breakdown structure (RBS), which follows the WBS and ensures that each activity and task in the WBS is reviewed for risk and opportunity and documented as identified. For example, in your restaurant planning, you have an activity and a group of tasks for implementing a sophisticated order entry system to communicate with the kitchen, the customer, and the credit card companies. The system will feed accounting and ordering information. Several excellent systems exist in the marketplace, and you know you need to pick the right one and ensure that it is installed in time for staff training and the grand opening. This is primarily a technical item from an outside vendor to be evaluated for external influences. Divide by deliverable, and identify responsible team members for each task. They, in turn, meet with the stakeholders, requesting the deliverable, defining requirements, and reviewing what could go wrong during your tasks and how you can react to them and manage them. This defines your risks. From this, you understand what needs to be monitored, who will be responsible for risk responses, and how you will resolve the issues generated. Not only does this help you prepare for risks but also leads you through more of the conversation about requirements and implementation strategies. This whole process is vital to the restaurant plans and success.

## Risk Response Strategies

The risk management responses are listed and defined.

## Image description

The risk management responses are described. The first, avoidance, is defined by eradicating the risk by eliminating the cause of the risk event. The second, mitigation, is defined by reducing the impact and probability of the risk event to an acceptable level. The third, transfer, is defined by transferring the risk and loss exposure to a third party. The last, acceptance, is defined by retaining the risk and developing plans to cover the consequences. The strategies for responding to risks identified in the planning process fall into three categories. You try to avoid the outcome, transfer the responsibility, or mitigate the problem (reducing the impact where practical and possible). There is a fourth but sparingly used strategy, which is to accept the outcome. This is usually only good for less critical and low overall impact risks. Here is a description of the responses: Risk avoidance—During the planning, the team identifies the risk and sets a path to avoid the risk. This could include additional costs or a change to the project scope. Often, specific functions required by the project are changed or eliminated during this planning phase, or additional funding is required to put stopgaps in place to avoid the risk from happening. You

examine the scope and functions, time, and budget to describe the impact of the risk and the change to avoid it. This requires change control and an approval processes to be in place. You could purchase your kitchen equipment from a regional manufacturer at a higher cost or purchase lower-cost equipment from an overseas supplier but risk shipping delays not meeting your project’s schedule. Transfer of the risk to someone else is the second option. It must be a risk that an insurance company wants to accept or an outside provider or party is willing to take responsibility for from the team. Often, costs are increased during this transfer, which may protect the scope but increase the budget. It is assumed that the third party has capabilities or financial resources beyond those found in the project to handle the outcomes should the risk occur. A good example of this is signing a fixed-price contract with a software system provider. Assume that the provider has the experience to avoid or mitigate the results if the risk occurs. The provider of your order entry software does this on a fixed-price contract because their experience reduces the risks of bad software performance during installation, causing cost overruns that you would otherwise absorb. Use of a fixed-price contract requires the provider to absorb cost overruns. They have installed the software in many restaurants and are confident enough to accept the transfer of the risk. Mitigation is when the team acts in some fashion to reduce the impact or likelihood of the risk occurring. By making an early investment or taking an early action, the team can minimize the impact of the risk should it occur. An example could be buying kitchen equipment from two suppliers rather than everything from one supplier so that if one happens to deliver late, the other equipment could proceed to installation rather than delaying all work. Splitting the equipment order between two suppliers may increase the price due to volume discounts. The additional cost would need to be included in the work activity budget. Accepting the risk is a response strategy where you recognize the risk and say that it is a part of the project or normal business practice, and the team will plan to accept it accordingly. Often, this is done where the risk is low or the impact is low and it is cheaper to accept the risk rather than spend on avoiding it. In the restaurant example where you have an operational service requirement for signage to meet the city requirements, you choose a small, local painter and hope they will complete the task according to your schedule. The city requires simple signage and that your building address is clearly marked. If you do not have the signs on time, you can still open. Therefore, you choose not to spend extra to speed the process. The risk is documented and acknowledged, and no action is taken. It is recommended that the sign

painter be monitored and coached accordingly. You accept the risk and do not spend additional time or money on this issue.

## Opportunity Management

There are some risks you face that are opportunities for the betterment of the project. In these cases, look to acknowledge the possibility and identify the upside (benefit) and encourage the opportunity to happen. Acceptance of the opportunity is a given. Should it happen, you will welcome it and use the benefits to your advantage. Often, the first weeks of a restaurant opening are slow and full of problems needing solutions. You budget for low revenue and are surprised when the neighborhood greets you with open arms and floods in. The revenue far exceeds your expectations. Enhancing the opportunity would be worth an investment if you think you can take action to make certain the opportunity happens. You can invest in the opportunity if you think the benefit will more probably happen and will exceed the investment. You can do this by assigning your best talent to be sure it happens. For example, you know that a restaurant critic will come into a new restaurant in the first week. Your hostess knows who this person is by sight. You advise her to put the critic at your best waiter’s table and tell the chef when the person arrives. She is to do this without informing anyone else in the restaurant and to avoid showing you know the critic is there. In this way, you will do your best to make the critic comfortable and look forward to a good review. Sharing the opportunity with your partners will help everyone benefit from the opportunity. If you know that the possible positive outcomes will benefit both the project and the partners, you will share the costs and benefits by sharing your information and knowledge with the partner. For instance, you know that a particular menu item is popular in the summer. You do an advertising campaign with your supplier to attract customers. This benefits you both. In summary, it is critical that you identify risks and opportunities and plan for actions to enhance the management of the risk or enhance the outcomes. The project manager is responsible for identifying, analyzing, communicating about, planning for, and monitoring all possible risks and

opportunities. Often, the items are added to the resource responsibility guide to account for responsibility and resource utilization. All of the risks reviewed and responded to take up time and money.
