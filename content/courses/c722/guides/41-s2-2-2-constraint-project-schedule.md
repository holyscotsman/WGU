# S2 2.2 Constraint- Project Schedule

## Learning Objectives

Define the project term triple constraint.

The project schedule, in its simplest form, is defined by its beginning and end dates. These are the dates you mark on your calendar. The project end date may be mandated by the customer or project sponsor, or it could be your best plan or "guess." Reality often changes that timeline, but more on that later. The project schedule contains much more information than just a proposed start and end date. It can be represented as a timeline on a calendar. Certain key dates (milestones) must be identified. These milestone dates indicate critical events and may include scheduled customer reviews, government inspections, and other activities that must be performed on specific dates before the project may proceed. The project will have activities that will each have their own start and end dates. Sometimes two or more activities can or must be conducted simultaneously. Often activities are contingent on each other, and the next milestone cannot begin until another has ended. Depending on the nature and complexity of the project, the schedule can be quite complex. A previous section discussed several project scheduling techniques, but this is a review some of these techniques. A Gantt chart for a very simple project is shown in the following figure. Here you see that 10 tasks (A through J) are listed as rows in the table. The duration of each task is provided, and the predecessors are identified. A predecessor task is one that must be completed before the next task can begin. In this example, you see that Task A is a predecessor task for Tasks B, C, and D.

A Gantt chart has rows of names, durations, and predecessors. The serial numbering begins with 1 and ends with 10.
