What Is Sociology?

Have you ever wondered how some of your colleagues and peers have built extensive personal and professional networks that help them get ahead, while others struggle to navigate school or workplace environments? Have you ever wondered why people in India often wear white to funerals, while Americans tend to wear black? These questions and many others can be answered through the study of sociology.

This graphic presents a definition of sociology. It includes the term sociology followed by a description explaining that it is the study of groups, group interactions, societies, and social interactions. An icon of three connected figures visually represents relationships and social connections.

Because societies are a central focus of sociology, sociologists define a society as a group of people who live in a defined geographic area, interact with one another, and share common practices, beliefs, and values.

Key takeaway: Sociology is the scientific and systematic study of groups and group interactions, societies, and social interactions.

Why Learn About Sociology?

Sociology can be exciting because it teaches people ways to recognize how they fit into the world and how others perceive them. Looking at themselves and society from a sociological perspective helps people see where they connect to different groups based on the many different ways they classify themselves and how society classifies them in turn. For example, a person may choose not to go to the doctor because they "do not like hospitals," but using the sociological perspective, a sociologist may point out how lack of insurance, transportation, paid time off, or trust in medical institutions influences that decision.

Key takeaway: The sociological perspective is the lens sociologists (and those studying sociology) use to examine social life and society.

## Sociology As a Science

Instead of relying on opinion or common sense, sociologists approach the study of society using systematic research methods to collect and analyze data. They then use that data to identify patterns in social life. This scientific approach allows sociologists to explain how social forces (societal patterns and structures) influence behavior in consistent ways. For example, if many students struggle in school, sociology looks beyond individual effort to examine factors such as school funding, neighborhood resources, and access to support services.

How Does Sociology Differ from Other Social Sciences?

Sociology is closely linked with other social sciences, such as psychology (the study of the mind and behavior) and anthropology (the study of humans and human cultures, past and present), but its focus is different.

Sociology focuses on society and groups by examining the lives and work of everyday people and how people are influenced by factors outside themselves, such as family, school, work, laws, and cultural expectations.

Psychology focuses on the individual. It looks at how people think, feel, and behave on an individual level.

Anthropology focuses on culture. It studies how people live in different societies, often comparing traditions, beliefs, and daily practices across cultures.

## How Sociologists Study Society

The shift in sociologists' viewpoint from a personal problem to a broader social context is referred to as the sociological imagination. The sociological imagination is the ability to connect personal experiences to broader social structures and historical forces (including culture). Sociologist C. Wright Mills introduced this idea to help people see how their lives are influenced by society, history, and culture. This way of thinking encourages sociologists to ask not just "What is happening to the individual?" but "What is happening in society that helps explain this?" When sociologists ask these questions, they can begin to distinguish between personal troubles, such as an individual being unemployed, and public issues, such as the percentage of the population who may be unemployed.

When studying situations like Bob's and Yuriko's, sociologists look beyond individual experiences to identify broader social patterns. While each person's story feels unique, sociologists ask whether similar outcomes appear among others living in the same society and facing similar social pressures. If many middle-aged workers in a region are unemployed after industries relocate, or if many working parents struggle with school pickup times due to work schedules, those individual experiences reflect larger social trends. This practice is called seeing the "general in the particular." It means recognizing general patterns in what initially appear to be individual outcomes.

Instructor's note: As you continue in this course, try using your sociological imagination to place the everyday experiences you learn about within a broader social context. And by seeing the general in the particular, you may be able to recognize how individual outcomes are shaped by the wider patterns and structures of society.

## The History of the Field of Sociology

Sociology emerged during a time of rapid social change in the 19th century. Industrialization transformed societies by moving large numbers of people from rural areas into cities, changing how people worked and lived. These changes created new social problems, such as overcrowding, poverty, and poor working conditions.

As traditional ways of life were disrupted, scholars began to question how societies functioned and how social order could be maintained. Rather than relying on philosophy or tradition alone, these thinkers wanted to apply scientific methods to the study of society. This shift laid the foundation for sociology as a distinct field focused on understanding social patterns and social change.

## Key Figures in the Field of Sociology

Early sociologists believed that society could be studied systematically, much like the natural sciences. They were interested in identifying patterns in social life and understanding how social forces influenced behavior. These thinkers focused on questions such as the following:

How do societies stay stable or change over time?

What causes social conflict?

How do social institutions shape people's lives?

## Auguste Comte (1798–1857)

## Auguste Comte (1798–1857)

Auguste Comte is considered the founder of sociology. He is credited with introducing the term sociology, which refers to the scientific study of society and social patterns. Comte argued that society could be studied scientifically, which he called positivism, and believed that identifying patterns in social life could help improve society.

As the founder of sociology, Auguste Comte proposed applying the rigorous scientific method, used in the physical sciences, to study human society. He aimed to discover social laws based on evidence rather than speculation.

## Harriet Martineau (1802–1876)

## Harriet Martineau (1802–1876)

Harriet Martineau helped introduce sociology to the English-speaking world by translating Auguste Comte’s work from French into English. She emphasized the importance of studying everyday life, specifically domestic life, including family roles and social inequality. Martineau believed sociologists should examine how social conditions affect different groups in unequal ways, particularly the lack of women’s rights.

## Karl Marx (1818–1883)

## Karl Marx (1818–1883)

Karl Marx focused on social conflict, which refers to tension and struggle between groups with unequal access to power and resources. He argued that society is shaped by conflict between social classes, particularly between those who own resources and those who work for them. Marx’s ideas influenced the conflict approach, a sociological perspective that examines inequality and competition within society.

## Herbert Spencer (1820–1903)

## Herbert Spencer (1820–1903)

Herbert Spencer applied ideas from biology to society and believed societies evolve over time. Social evolution refers to the idea that societies develop and change gradually through natural processes. Spencer argued that social institutions develop naturally and supported a hands-off approach to social change. Borrowing from Charles Darwin, Spencer said the concept of survival of the fittest applied to human society as well, stating that it was natural for some people to be poor and others to be rich. His ideas became known as Social Darwinism.

## Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

## Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

Émile Durkheim emphasized the importance of social facts, which are patterns of behavior and ways of thinking that exist outside individuals and influence their actions. Examples of social facts include laws, norms, and shared values. Durkheim believed sociologists should study these patterns scientifically to understand how societies maintain order.

His well-known book Suicide: A Study in Sociology highlighted how much society and social integration can influence even this most seemingly individual act. In 1897, Durkheim attempted to demonstrate the effectiveness of his rules of social research when he published Suicide: A Study in Sociology. Durkheim examined suicide statistics in different police districts to identify differences between Catholic and Protestant communities.

Similar to many early sociologists, Durkheim researched differences between traditional and modern societies. He felt that people in both societies were connected with each other, but how they were connected differed. Mechanical solidarity was found in traditional societies in which people were connected by their shared beliefs, values, and similar work, allowing individuals to fill in for one another because their work tasks were alike.

## Jane Addams (1860–1935)

## Jane Addams (1860–1935)

Jane Addams combined sociological research with social action, which involves using sociological knowledge to address social problems. Her research allowed her to advocate for immigrant rights, child labor laws, and women’s suffrage.

She worked directly with communities to improve living and working conditions and believed understanding society required engagement with real people and real conditions.

## Max Weber (1864–1920)

## Max Weber (1864–1920)

Max Weber is best known for his book The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, which analyzes the relationship between the economy and religion. Weber was interested in studying how economic transition led to rationalization and the development of bureaucratic organizations.

Weber was critical of rationalization, believing it created an “Iron Cage” of systems in which people could become trapped in rigid bureaucratic processes.

## W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963)

## W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963)

W.E.B. Du Bois, the first Black man to earn a doctorate from Harvard, studied race, inequality, and identity. He introduced the concept of double consciousness, which describes the experience of viewing oneself through multiple social identities at the same time, particularly in the context of race. Du Bois emphasized using sociology to understand and address social injustice, which contributed to the founding of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP).

## Sociological Perspectives and Theories

Sociologists do not all study society in the same way. Instead, they use sociological perspectives, which are lenses or ways of looking at society. Perspectives represent different vantage points for studying the social world. Sociologists use multiple perspectives but recognize that neither perspective is correct or incorrect.

Sociologists build theories within these perspectives to help explain what they observe. A theory is an explanation for how and why social life works the way it does, and it can be used to create a testable idea. In other words, theories help sociologists move beyond broad perspectives and use evidence to support their explanations.

What Is the Functionalist Perspective?

The functionalist perspective (also called structural functionalism) is a macro-level way of looking at society. The functionalist perspective looks at society as a system where different parts or institutions work together to keep society running smoothly. For example, schools (which are part of the social institution of education) teach students math, reading, and skills that a healthy labor force needs. They also teach rules, teamwork, and responsibility. From a functionalist perspective, it is in these ways that schools ensure society has what it needs to maintain stability: an educated and prepared workforce and citizenry who share a common culture.

What Do Functionalists Study?

From a functionalist perspective, sociologists study how social institutions contribute to society. Functionalists are especially interested in questions like these:

How does a social institution help society meet important needs?

How do institutions work together to maintain order and stability?

What happens when an institution stops working effectively?

## Unintended and Intended Outcomes

Why do people go to college? What else happens in college that may not be part of the official goal? These are questions that a sociologist would ask from the functionalist perspective.

Functionalists recognize that social institutions, such as educational systems, often produce more than one outcome. Some outcomes are planned and expected or intended (referred to as manifest functions), while others happen unexpectedly and are unintended (referred to as latent functions). ## Critique of the Functionalist Perspective

One major criticism of the functionalist perspective is that it does not do a good job explaining how or why societies change over time. Another issue is that the theory can be circular; it assumes that repeated behaviors must have a purpose, but it only identifies their purpose because they are repeated.

In addition, some behaviors or institutions continue even when they no longer serve a helpful role, which goes against the theory's basic idea that everything exists because it has a function. Because of these problems, many sociologists think functionalism is not very useful as a big picture (macro- level) explanation anymore, though it can still be helpful when studying society at a more specific, midlevel scale.

What Is the Conflict Perspective?

The conflict perspective is a macro-level way of looking at society. From the conflict perspective, society is not always balanced or fair. This perspective is most closely associated with Karl Marx, who argued that society is shaped by conflict between groups competing for scarce resources, such as wealth, education, housing, healthcare, power, and political influence.

## Example of the Conflict Perspective

Look at an example of the conflict perspective. Conflict theorists might explain differences in opportunity by pointing to unequal access to resources such as education, housing, and high-paying jobs. Some groups have access to more resources; they are more likely to attend better-funded schools, live in safer neighborhoods, and gain access to stronger job networks. Over time, these advantages can continue across generations, even when individuals work equally hard.

What Is a Resource?

In the conflict perspective, a resource is anything valuable that people or groups compete for because it helps them meet needs, gain advantages, or increase power. Resources can include the following: money and wealth; jobs and income; education and credentials; housing and safe neighborhoods; healthcare; political influence and decision-making power; time and leisure.

Key takeaway: The conflict perspective is a macro-level viewpoint that explains society as shaped by unequal power and competition between groups for scarce resources, which creates and maintains unequal opportunities over time.

## Conflict Perspective's View of Society

From the conflict perspective, society is shaped by inequality between groups and power differences. They often describe society in terms of dominant groups, who have more power and resources, and subordinate groups, who have less power and fewer opportunities. Because resources are limited, groups compete for things like money, education, housing, and political influence. Conflict theorists also argue that social institutions can help maintain these unequal outcomes over time, but conflict can also lead to social change when groups challenge inequality.

## Example

For example, the conflict perspective might explain differences in educational success by focusing on how unequal access to resources creates unequal opportunities. If some schools receive more funding, that funding leads to better facilities, better learning resources, and more support staff. Students in these schools may have more advantages than students in schools with fewer resources.

## Critique of the Conflict Perspective

The conflict perspective is often criticized for focusing too heavily on conflict. Because it highlights disagreement and struggle so strongly, it can overlook the fact that many social structures remain stable over long periods of time or change slowly and gradually rather than suddenly, as conflict theory predicts.

## Conflict Perspective: Feminist Theory

One important modern theory that falls within the conflict perspective is the feminist theory. This theory focuses on gender inequality and how overcoming inequality requires social change because it is built into social structures. Feminist theorists examine how power and privilege are connected to gender and how these patterns can shape people's opportunities and experiences.

This diagram highlights the conflict perspective within sociological perspectives and shows feminist theory as an example connected to it. It illustrates how a specific theory fits within a broader sociological perspective.

## Feminist Theorists' View of Society

Feminist theorists argue that gender inequality is not a personal issue or an individual problem, nor is it accidental. Rather, it is built into long-standing social power structures. For example, workplace leadership positions are often disproportionately held by men. This pattern is not simply the result of individual choices but reflects broader expectations, hiring practices, and promotion systems that advantage men over women.

One key idea in feminist theory is patriarchy, a social system in which men, as a group, hold more power and authority than women. This unequal distribution of power shapes expectations, opportunities, and outcomes in areas such as work, family roles, education, and leadership. A key distinction between feminist theory and conflict perspective is that the former calls for action steps to better the lives of women through policies and institutional practices.

## What Is the Symbolic Interactionist

Perspective?

The symbolic interactionist perspective (also called symbolic interactionism) is a micro-level way of looking at society. This means it focuses on small-scale, everyday interactions between individuals, such as conversations, relationships, and communication through texting or social media. Symbolic interactionists study how people use symbols (e.g., words, gestures, emojis) to create shared meaning in daily life.

For example, consider a short text message reply that simply says, "K." One person might interpret it as neutral, while another might see it as rude or annoyed. A symbolic interactionist would focus on how the meaning of the message depends on the relationship between the people, the situation, and the shared expectations about what that symbol (the message) means.

## Key Contributors to the Symbolic

## Interactionist Perspective

One important contributor to symbolic interactionism is George Herbert Mead, who is considered a founder of the symbolic interactionist perspective. Mead focused on how people create society through social interaction and shared meaning, specifically through the development of the self.

Another key contributor is Herbert Blumer, a student of Mead. Blumer coined the term symbolic interactionism and explained observable interactions, or how people interact based on the meanings things have for them, and those meanings come from social interaction.

Key takeaway: The symbolic interactionist perspective is a micro-level approach that explains social life by examining how people use and interpret symbols (like words, gestures, and emojis) to create meaning in everyday interactions.

## Critique of the Symbolic Interactionist

## Perspective

Research using this perspective is often questioned because it can be hard for researchers to stay completely objective. Others argue that the focus on symbolic interaction is too narrow and ignores how social structures affect human behavior and choices.

## Symbolic Interactionist Perspective: Social

## Exchange Theory

An important modern-day theory from the symbolic interactionist perspective is the social exchange theory. This theory argues that society is shaped by ongoing interactions in which individuals seek to maximize rewards and minimize costs. It assumes that people evaluate the options available to them and try to choose the one that provides the greatest benefit with the least loss.

This diagram presents the symbolic interactionist perspective within sociological perspectives and shows three related theories: social exchange theory, differential association theory, and labeling theory. It illustrates how multiple theories are connected to and support this perspective.

## Social Exchange Theory Formula

Social exchange theory is often summarized with a simple formula: Rewards − Costs = Outcome

In other words: "What I gain" − "What I lose" = "My decision."

## The Concept of Equity

Sometimes these decisions lead to positive outcomes, and sometimes they do not. A key concept in social exchange theory is equity, or the sense that an interaction is fair to everyone involved based on the rewards and costs exchanged. For example, in some dual-income households where both partners work full time, women may still perform more housework and childcare than men. Even if the arrangement appears unequal, it may be perceived as fair by the individuals involved due to expectations about roles, conflict avoidance, or beliefs about competence. In these cases, the relationship continues because both partners view the overall exchange as acceptable.

## Applied Sociology

Sociology is useful because it helps people understand how society works and why patterns exist in everyday life. However, sociology is not only used to explain society, but it can also be used to improve it. When sociologists take what they know about society and use it to address real-world issues, they are practicing applied sociology.

Outcomes of applied sociology include the following:

solving problems in areas such as education, healthcare, work, family life, and communities

identifying practical strategies that can make life better for individuals and groups

identifying barriers that affect people's opportunities and using research to recommend changes

## Example of Applied Sociology

To see how applied sociology works, consider the scenario of a student who is attending a traditional university while working full time. If the student's work schedule changes suddenly, they may miss deadlines, fall behind, and feel overwhelmed. From a sociological perspective, this situation is not just about one student's time management. It may also be connected to larger social systems such as workplace expectations, education policies, access to technology, and family responsibilities. An applied sociologist might use the conflict perspective to examine how unequal access to resources, such as time, reliable internet, and flexible work schedules, creates barriers for some students but not others.

Based on this analysis, applied sociology might recommend solutions such as creating online educational environments that provide affordable, high-quality education that better supports working students. WGU was founded when several state governors realized the traditional model of education was not working for their constituents and designed a new model of education to serve a more diverse and underserved student population. These types of changes focus on reducing barriers in the system rather than blaming the individual, helping more students succeed.

## Clinical Sociology

Clinical sociology is a type of applied sociology that focuses on improving social situations through analysis and intervention. Clinical sociologists study social problems and then work directly with individuals, groups, or organizations to create change by identifying solutions and supporting improvement through interventions. Clinical sociology can be used in settings such as schools, workplaces, healthcare organizations, and social service agencies.

## Clinical Sociology Example

A community clinic notices that many patients delay treatment because they cannot afford doctor visits or medications. A sociologist would study the situation to determine the barriers that prevent people from getting care. Then, they would work with the clinic and community partners to create an intervention, such as connecting patients to financial assistance programs, improving access to low- cost medication options, and helping patients schedule appointments outside of standard work hours.

## Comparing Applied and Clinical Sociology

Applied sociology uses sociological research to identify and reduce real-world social barriers through practical solutions, while clinical sociology is a hands-on form of applied sociology that directly intervenes with individuals, groups, or organizations to improve social conditions. ## Steps in the Research Process

Sociologists use a structured research process to investigate patterns in social life and explain why patterns happen in society. This process is based on the scientific method, which Comte, the founder of sociology, originally proposed. The scientific method helps sociologists stay organized, objective, and consistent as they collect and analyze information about the social world.

The scientific method typically includes six main steps, shown in the following graphic: "The Research Process." This infographic outlines the six steps of the research process in a vertical sequence. The steps include asking a question, conducting background research, constructing a hypothesis, designing and conducting a study to collect data, analyzing the data, and reporting the results. Each step is paired with an icon and connected by arrows to show progression.

## Applying the Steps in the Research

## Process

The following is an example of how a sociologist applied the steps of the research process.

## Step 1: Ask a Question

Imagine a sociologist notices that crime rates are increasing in some communities and not others. Instead of assuming the cause, the sociologist begins the research process by asking a question:

How do social and economic conditions affect differences in crime rates across neighborhoods and cities?

## Step 2: Conduct Background Research

Next, the sociologist reviews existing research and background information to see what other researchers have already discovered about crime rates and social and economic conditions. This includes reading studies, examining crime statistics, and looking at existing records from sources such as police databases or court systems.

## Step 3: Construct a Hypothesis

After reviewing what is already known, the sociologist forms a hypothesis, which is a prediction about what they expect to find. For example, the sociologist might predict: If unemployment increases, then the crime rate will increase.

Step 4: Design and Conduct a Study (Collect the

## Data)

To test this hypothesis, the sociologist designs a study and collects data. This might include using existing data sources on unemployment and crime rates or gathering additional information through surveys or other research methods. (For additional information about research methods, see page 3.2.)

## Step 5: Analyze the Data

Once the data is collected, the sociologist analyzes it to look for patterns and determine whether the results support the hypothesis.

## Step 6: Report Results

Finally, the sociologist reports the findings by sharing the results in an article, a book, a report to the funding body of the research, or a professional conference. This allows other researchers and stakeholders to review the study so they can build on the research in the future and apply the knowledge to future policy development.

Key takeaway: Sociologists apply the six steps of the scientific method to systematically ask questions, test hypotheses, collect data, and report findings about patterns in social life.

## Purpose

Sociological research can have different purposes. Some studies describe what is happening in society. Others explain why it is happening.

## Descriptive Research

Descriptive research focuses on what is happening. For example, a sociologist might study how often people use social media, how many students work while in college, or differences in access to healthcare. It answers questions such as the following:

What is happening?

How common is this behavior?

## Explanatory Research

Explanatory research focuses on why something happens. It looks at relationships between causes and outcomes. For example, a sociologist might study why some communities have higher crime rates, why some students graduate at higher rates, or why certain groups have better health outcomes. It answers questions such as the following:

Why does this happen?

What factors influence this outcome?

Key takeaway: Descriptive research explains what is happening. Explanatory research explains why it is happening.

## Based on Data and Methods

Sociologists also choose research methods based on the type of data they collect. The two main approaches are quantitative research and qualitative research.

## Quantitative Research

Quantitative research uses numerical data that can be measured and analyzed statistically. It is useful for studying large groups and identifying broad patterns. For example, a sociologist might measure how many people have access to healthcare, how many students work while attending college, or whether tutoring improves grades.

## Qualitative Research

Qualitative research uses non-numerical data, such as interviews, observations, and written responses. It is useful for understanding people's experiences and perspectives. For example, a sociologist might interview students about how working affects their school experience or observe how families respond to stress.

## Choosing the Type of Data

Once sociologists develop a research question, they must decide how they will collect data to answer it. Data is information used to understand social behavior and patterns. Researchers must decide whether they will use primary data or secondary data. The key difference is who collected the data.

Primary data is data collected directly by the researcher or research team.

Secondary data is data that already exists and was collected by others outside of the current research.

## Method: Survey

Surveys collect information through questionnaires or interviews.

Advantage:

They have the ability to collect data from large numbers of people.

Disadvantage:

People may not answer honestly or provide much detail about a topic.

## Method: Experiments

Experiments test cause and effect under controlled conditions.

Advantage:

They are replicable by other researchers and can prove cause and effect.

Disadvantage:

Not all research questions are suitable for this type of study.

## Method: Field Research

Field research takes place in natural settings.

Advantage:

gives in-depth information

Disadvantage:

may lack generalizability (ability to generalize to the large population) as it relies on context

## Method: Participant Observation

Participant observation involves the researcher joining a group while observing it.

Advantage:

You can gain an insider's perspective as a researcher.

Disadvantage:

This can be an ethical concern, since the researcher can lose objectivity.

## Method: Ethnography

Ethnography involves long-term immersion in a group or culture.

Advantage:

extremely rich and detailed data

Disadvantages:

can be time-consuming, subject to interpretation by the researcher, and lack generalizability to a larger population

## Method: Case Study

A case study focuses on one individual, group, or event.

Advantage:

useful for unique cases and building future research questions

Disadvantages:

limited generalizability to a larger population

## Method: Content Analysis

Content analysis examines media or documents for patterns and themes.

Advantage:

an unobtrusive way to collect data

Disadvantages:

can be time-consuming; content is subject to interpretation Method: Secondary Data Analysis

Secondary data analysis uses data that already exists. For example, census data can be used to examine how education levels differ across regions or how income inequality has changed over time.

Advantage:

saves time and money; access to extensive data collections

Disadvantage:

may not be an existing data source that matches specifically what you need

## The Importance of Ethics in Research

Sociologists study real people and real communities, which means research can have real consequences. They follow ethical standards to protect participants from harm and ensure that research is conducted responsibly. One of the most well-known examples of unethical research is the Tuskegee Syphilis Study, in which 600 Black people were misled about their diagnosis and denied treatment so researchers could observe the effects of untreated illness over time. This case highlights why ethical guidelines are essential in sociological research.

## Research Integrity

In addition to protecting participants, sociologists must also maintain research integrity, which means conducting and reporting research honestly and responsibly. Research integrity helps ensure that sociological findings are accurate, trustworthy, and useful for understanding society.

Key expectations include the following:

Proper attribution: Plagiarism occurs when a researcher uses someone else's work or ideas without giving proper credit. Sociologists must always cite sources and clearly acknowledge the contributions of others.

Expertise: Researchers must stay within their area of training and knowledge. Sociologists should not claim credentials, skills, or expertise they do not have.

Conflict of interest: A conflict of interest occurs when personal, financial, or professional interests could influence the research process or the results. Sociologists must avoid situations where bias can affect the study.

Accurate reporting of results: Ethical researchers report results honestly, even if the findings do not support their hypothesis or expectations. Sociologists should not omit, distort, or change results to match a desired outcome.

Key takeaway: To ensure that sociological research is credible and ethical, researchers are expected to follow professional standards that guide how studies are conducted and reported.

## Lesson 3: Summary

Take a moment to think about what you learned in this lesson.

Sociologists use descriptive research to identify and describe social patterns, and they use explanatory research to explain why those patterns happen.

Sociological research can be quantitative (focused on numbers and measurable data) or qualitative (focused on meanings, experiences, and detailed descriptions).

The sociological research process follows these steps using the scientific method:

1. asking a question

2. reviewing existing research

3. forming a hypothesis

4. designing and conducting a study

5. analyzing results

6. reporting findings

Sociologists collect data using research methods such as experiments, surveys, field research, content analysis, and secondary data analysis.

Research findings can be affected by data integrity issues, such as missing information, biased results, or limitations in how data are collected and interpreted.

Ethical research requires protecting participants through practices such as informed consent, confidentiality, and avoiding exploitation, especially when working with vulnerable groups.

Research integrity also depends on ethical professional behavior, including avoiding plagiarism, staying within areas of expertise, preventing conflicts of interest, and reporting results accurately.
