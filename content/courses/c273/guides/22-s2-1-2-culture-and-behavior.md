## Biological Approach to Behavior (Nature)

Biological behaviors are innate, natural, and inherent from birth. These predispositions are common across all humans, regardless of where someone lives or what culture they belong to.

For example, people experience hunger when they need food and become tired when they need sleep (drives). People also tend to react quickly to danger, such as pulling their hand away from something hot (reflex). These behaviors do not have to be taught; they happen because of the way the human body works.

This infographic presents three categories of behavior: reflexes, instincts, and drives. Each category is shown in a separate panel with a title, brief definition, and example. Reflexes are described as automatic physical reactions with the example of a knee jerk reflex. Instincts are defined as inborn survival behaviors, illustrated by a baby crying for care. Drives are explained as internal needs that motivate behavior, with examples including hunger, thirst, and sleep.

Sociological Approach to Behavior

(Nurture)

In contrast to behaviors people are born with, nurture refers to learned behaviors, which are actions that humans develop from the environment around them. For example, riding public transportation involves expected behaviors that people learn, such as finding a marked bus stop or station, waiting for the bus or train, paying before or after boarding, and sitting quietly if a seat is available.

These expectations can also differ by location. In some places, passengers might board while the bus is still moving, while in other places (nurture) riders are expected to signal the bus to stop by extending an arm. These differences show how behavior is influenced by social learning and shared expectations rather than being automatic or the same everywhere.

## How Nurture Affects Nature

Sociologists examine how environmental factors such as poverty, food security, or

access to healthcare can affect biological outcomes. For example, a pregnant

woman experiencing poverty without access to healthy foods, prenatal vitamins, or

prenatal care is less likely to have a healthy pregnancy compared to a wealthy

woman who is able to afford organic food, take prenatal vitamins, and attend all

recommended prenatal visits.

Key takeaway: Human behavior is shaped by both biological factors (nature),

such as reflexes, instincts, and drive, and social factors (nurture), such as shared

cultural practices and environmental factors that can shape biological outcomes.
