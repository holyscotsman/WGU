# S3 2 Summary

## Lesson 2: Summary

Take a moment to think about what you learned in this lesson. Social groups vary in size, structure, and purpose, which affects how members interact and how stable the group is over time. Primary groups are small, personal, and emotionally close, while secondary groups are larger, more goal-oriented, and less personal. Group size matters: dyads are highly intimate but unstable, while triads and larger groups tend to be more stable but less personal. Individuals occupy statuses within a group or society, and the expected behaviors attached to those statuses are called roles. People often hold multiple statuses at once (a status set), but one status may become especially influential as a master status. Leadership affects how groups focus. Instrumental leaders focus on tasks and goal achievement. Expressive leaders focus on morale, cohesion, and relationships. Groups also differ by leadership style: Authoritarian: The leader makes decisions with little group input. Democratic: The leader encourages participation and shared decision-making. Laissez-faire: The leader provides minimal direction; members make many decisions independently. Group dynamics can lead to challenges, referred to as group phenomena. Conformity occurs when individuals adjust their behavior to fit group norms. Groupthink occurs when the desire for agreement discourages critical thinking. Social loafing occurs when individuals contribute less effort in a group. Social dilemmas occur when personal interests conflict with a group's goal. Formal organizations are structured to achieve specific goals and operate through defined rules and roles. Three types of formal organizations, based on how they motivate members to join, include utilitarian, voluntary, and coercive. Bureaucracies rely on hierarchy, written rules, communication, division of labor, and interchangeability, which can improve efficiency but also lead to dysfunctions such as red tape.
