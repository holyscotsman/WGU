## Group Size and Group Dynamics

Think about working on a group project. If there are only two people, everyone's contribution is easy to see, and communication is direct. As more people join a group, coordination becomes harder. People begin to form roles, and the group may create rules or choose leaders. Sociologists use group size—the number of people in a group—to help explain these differences.

The smallest possible group is a dyad, a group of two people. Dyads tend to be highly personal and emotionally intense because each member relies heavily on the other. However, dyads are also less stable because the group cannot continue if one person leaves.

A triad is a group of three people. Triads are more stable than dyads because the group can continue even if one person leaves. At the same time, adding a third member can change group interaction by allowing alliances or majority opinions to form.

Does Group Size Matter?

As groups become larger, they are generally more stable but less intimate. Members may not know each other well, and communication often becomes more formal.

Larger groups typically need clearer roles, rules, or leadership to function effectively, which increases the level of organization within the group.

## The Impact of Group Leadership

Think about a manager, coach, or instructor you have worked with. How effective were they as leaders? Sociologists study group leadership to understand how leaders guide group members, shape behavior, and influence how groups reach goals.

Types of Leaders: What They Are Trying to Accomplish?

Sociologists identify different types of leaders based on what the leader focuses on or is trying to accomplish. (Scholars sometimes refer to this as the leadership function.) There are two approaches: instrumental and expressive. Many effective leaders combine both functions depending on the situation.

An instrumental leader focuses on completing tasks and achieving goals. They prioritize efficiency and results.

An expressive leader focuses on group morale and emotional well-being. They emphasize support, cooperation, and positive relationships.

## Leadership Styles: How They Make Decisions

Group leaders also differ in leadership styles, or how they make decisions. The following are three leadership styles:

Democratic leaders encourage group participation and seek input before acting.

Authoritarian leaders make decisions with little group input and expect compliance.

Laissez-faire leaders take a hands-off approach, allowing group members to make most decisions themselves.

Maybe one person put in less effort because others were picking up the slack, or no one spoke up even though they disagreed with the group's decision. Sociologists refer to these recurring patterns as group phenomena.

## Social Loafing

One common group phenomenon is social loafing—individuals putting in less effort when working as part of a group than when working alone. This often happens because individual contributions are harder to identify in group settings. For example, in a large group project, some members may rely on others to do most of the work. Social loafing is generally perceived as negative.

## Social Dilemma

A social dilemma occurs when individuals must choose between what benefits them personally and what benefits society. For example, someone may use more water than allowed during a drought or go to work sick to avoid losing hourly wages. Social dilemmas highlight the tension between individual goals and collective responsibility.

## Group Conformity

Conformity is adjusting behavior or beliefs to match social norms. For example, an employee may go along with a team's approach during a meeting even if they disagree. Conformity can help groups function smoothly, but it can also discourage independent thinking.

## Groupthink

Irving Janis coined the term groupthink to describe an extreme form of conformity. According to Janis, groupthink occurs when the desire for agreement prevents group members from critically evaluating ideas. This can lead to poor decisions and a lack of accountability.

dyad: a group consisting of two people
