## Communicating with Symbols

People use symbols to communicate ideas and make sense of the world around them. Recall that a symbol is anything that carries a shared meaning recognized by people within a culture. Symbols can include objects, images, gestures, sounds, or words. On their own, symbols have no meaning. Their meaning comes from the shared understanding people attach to them.

Symbols help people interpret social situations quickly. For example, a stop sign communicates the need to stop without requiring spoken instructions. A wedding ring can signal marital status, and a school logo can represent identity and belonging. These meanings are learned through social interaction and are widely understood within a particular culture.

## Symbols Vary Across Societies

Because symbols are socially constructed, their meanings can vary across societies and over time. For example, a thumbs-up gesture is commonly used in the United States to signal approval or agreement. However, in some cultures, such as Russia and Australia, this same gesture can be interpreted as offensive. This difference shows that symbols only have meaning within a shared cultural context.

Key takeaway: Symbols are culturally shared meanings attached to objects, gestures, or words that help people communicate and interpret social situations quickly.

## Communicating with Language

Language is a shared system of words and signed symbols (such as American Sign Language, ASL) that people use to communicate meaning. Through language, people express ideas, share information, and coordinate social life. Language also helps pass culture from one generation to the next by teaching shared meanings and expectations.

Before language is shaped by culture, it can vary across societies and social groups. For example, in the United States, people may use different words for the same object depending on region, such as calling a carbonated drink soda or pop. Similarly, when leaving a restaurant, some people ask for the check, while others ask for the bill. These differences in word choice can cause brief confusion or signal regional or cultural differences in communication.

Key takeaway: Language is a culturally shaped system of spoken or signed symbols that people use to communicate, share meaning, and pass culture across generations. Being aware of language differences helps explain why misunderstandings sometimes occur, even when people speak the same language.

## Communicating with Gestures

Gestures are physical movements that communicate meaning without words and are a form of nonverbal communication. They are not part of a formal language system, such as American Sign Language (ASL). They are learned through social interaction and often support or replace spoken language.

Examples of common gestures used in the United States include the following:

nodding the head to indicate agreement

shaking the head to indicate disagreement

waving a hand to greet someone or say goodbye

pointing to direct attention to an object or location

smiling or frowning to express emotion

Because gestures are socially constructed, the same gesture can be interpreted differently across cultures, sometimes leading to misunderstanding. For example, maintaining direct eye contact is often interpreted as a sign of confidence or attentiveness in the United States. In some cultures, however, prolonged eye contact can be seen as disrespectful or confrontational.

## The Connection Between Material and Nonmaterial Culture

Material and nonmaterial aspects of culture are linked, and physical objects often symbolize cultural ideas. Review the following examples.

A metro pass is a material object, but it represents a form of nonmaterial culture, namely, capitalism, and the acceptance of paying for transportation.

Clothing, hairstyles, and jewelry are part of material culture, but the appropriateness of wearing certain clothing for specific events reflects nonmaterial culture.

A school building belongs to material culture symbolizing education, but the teaching methods and educational standards are part of education's nonmaterial culture.

Key takeaway: Material culture is expressed and understood through physical objects. Nonmaterial culture consists of the ideas, attitudes, and beliefs of a society. Understanding material culture helps you recognize how appearance, tools, and technology influence how others evaluate your professionalism, competence, and reliability.

## Key

language: a shared system of words and symbols used to communicate meaning

gestures: physical movements that communicate meaning without words

nonverbal communication: communication that occurs without spoken or written language

material culture: physical objects that communicate shared meaning, values, and expectations

nonmaterial culture: consists of the ideas, attitudes, and beliefs of a society
