## Changes in Marriage through History

Marriage in the United States has evolved through distinct historical phases. Before the Industrial Revolution, marriage was largely institutional, meaning it was formed primarily for economic survival. Families depended on shared labor, land, and property, and marriage strengthened economic alliances between kin groups. From roughly 1850 to 1965, marriage shifted toward a companionate model. Emotional intimacy, love, and personal connection became central expectations for the basis of marriage. Today, scholars describe marriage as increasingly expressive. Modern marriage in the United States is expected to provide personal fulfillment and help individuals reach their potential. Although economic factors still matter, marriage is now commonly framed as a relationship grounded in individual choice and self-development.

## Trends in Marriage Rates

## Marriage and Cohabitation

Marriage remains a central social institution in the United States, but participation has declined. In 2016, approximately 54.8% of Americans age 18 and older were married, compared to earlier decades. Cohabitation rates increased compared to previous generations.

## Explanations for Trends

Researchers have several explanations for these trends in marriage: Cultural changes: Cultural changes, such as decreased stigma surrounding cohabitation and having children outside of marriage, may reduce social pressure to marry and increase the cohabitation rates compared to previous generations. Marriages benefit men: Another sociological explanation surfaced in the 1970s, which is heavily supported by research today, and concludes that marriages benefit men more than women. Men report greater satisfaction in their marriages than women do, and men who are married are healthier than their unmarried counterparts and women (both married and unmarried). Economic security: Although both cultural and economic factors play a role, research consistently shows that economic security is one of the strongest predictors of marriage formation and marital stability. Personal growth: Individuals are waiting longer to marry as they pursue higher education, career development, and financial stability.

Key takeaway: Marriage in the 21st century occurs later and less frequently. Cohabitation rates are increasing. Explanations include cultural changes, benefits for men versus women, economic security, and personal growth.

## Trends in Divorce

Divorce rates increased sharply during the 1960s and 1970s before stabilizing in recent decades. Although divorce is common, it is not evenly distributed across social groups. Several sociodemographic factors affect an individual's risk for divorce. The following are referred to as protective factors:

- having a higher income
- having a baby 7 months or more after marriage
- getting married after 25 years of age
- having parents who are still married
- having a religious affiliation
- having a college degree

Key takeaway: Divorce rates are stabilizing, but they are not evenly distributed across social groups. Protective factors reduce the risk of divorce.

## The Marriage Gap

Marriage patterns vary across social groups, creating what scholars call the marriage gap, which is the mismatch in educational attainment between never-married men and never-married women. Marriage is often described as a personal choice, but patterns suggest it is also shaped by structural factors such as gender, education, and socioeconomic status. The term marriage gap is used to refer to two phenomena: College-educated and higher-income people are more likely to marry and to have more stable marriages Women with higher education experience a mismatch in the marriage market

Why the Gap? Scholars debate the primary cause of the marriage gap. Some emphasize economic instability, arguing that difficulty securing stable, well-paying employment discourages marriage, particularly among individuals with fewer educational credentials. Others point to the practice of homogamy, citing that heterosexual women with higher educational attainment are not finding a match with men. Key takeaway: The marriage gap shows that marriage in the 21st century is increasingly shaped by education and economic stability, with higher-income and more educated individuals more likely to marry and remain married than those facing economic insecurity.

## Types of Families in the 21st Century

The image of a married mother and father with children does not reflect most households today. Family life in the 21st century looks more varied than it did in the past. Many people still live in households headed by married couples, but it is also common to see partners who live together without marriage, one-parent households, blended families, gay and lesbian couples, and single people living on their own. These patterns matter because you cannot assume everyone has the same living arrangement or support system. Instead of thinking of family as just one structure, it helps to think about who shares a home, who shares responsibilities, and who provides emotional and financial support.

## Married couples

A married-couple household is led by two people who are legally married. Some married couples have children living with them, and some do not. Marriage is still an important institution in the United States, but fewer households today are headed by married couples than in previous generations. This shift affects everyday expectations. For example, you cannot assume a child lives with two married parents.

## Cohabitation

Cohabitation means two partners live together in a romantic relationship but are not legally married. Some cohabiting couples are raising children, and others are not. People may choose cohabitation to share living costs, to spend more time together, or as a step before marriage. Cohabitation has become more socially accepted over time. Cohabitation rates have increased significantly in the United States over the last two decades, marking a major shift in family structure. Researchers expect cohabitation rates to continue to rise, while rates of marriage decline.

## One-parent families

A one-parent family is a household where one parent is the main caregiver and adult in the home. This can happen because of divorce, separation, the death of a partner, or personal choice. Oneparent families can be stable and supportive, and they often involve one adult managing most parenting tasks, time demands, and financial responsibilities.

## Blended families

A blended family (also called a stepfamily) forms when at least one adult brings a child from a previous relationship into a new marriage or partnership. Blended families may include stepparents, stepsiblings, and half-siblings. They often need time to adjust to new roles, routines, and expectations, especially around rules, discipline, and family traditions.

## Same-sex couples

Families led by same sex couples may include children or may be adult-only households. Children may join the family through birth, adoption, foster care, or previous relationships. Over time, social acceptance has increased, and legal recognition has expanded, which affects access to benefits and protections in areas like healthcare, family leave, and parenting rights.
