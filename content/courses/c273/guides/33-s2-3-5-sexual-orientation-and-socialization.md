## Sexual Orientation

Categories related to sex and sexuality are often assumed to be biological or fixed. In addition to biological sex, sociologists examine sexual orientation, which refers to a person's physical, mental, emotional, and sexual attraction to a particular sex (male and/or female). Sociologists focus on patterns of attraction along with behavior. It is important to note that sexual orientation is distinct from sexual behavior, and people do not need to engage in sexual activity to understand or identify their orientation.

Categories of sexual orientation include the following:

Heterosexuality is the attraction to individuals of the opposite sex.

Homosexuality is the attraction to individuals of the same sex.

Bisexuality is the attraction to individuals of either sex.

Asexuality is a lack of sexual attraction or desire for sexual contact.

Pansexuality is gender blindness, or attraction to people regardless of sex, gender, gender identity, or gender expression.

Omnisexuality is an attraction to people of all sexes, genders, gender identities, gender expression identities, and gender expressions that considers the person's gender. Gender influences the attraction.

Queer is an umbrella term used to describe sexual orientation, gender identity, or gender expression that does not align with traditional categories.

Sociologists emphasize that attraction and identity do not exist as fixed or rigid

categories. It is important to acknowledge and understand that many of these

orientations exist on a spectrum, and there may be no specific term to describe

how an individual feels. Social context also matters, as sexuality and gender

terminology are constantly changing and may mean different things to different

people; they are not universal, and individuals define them for themselves.

Key takeaway: Sexual orientation refers to patterns of emotional, romantic, or

sexual attraction, not behavior, and sociologists understand these orientations as

diverse, fluid, and shaped by social context rather than fixed categories.

## Fluidity of Gender and Sexual Identity

Sociologists recognize that gender and sexuality are not fixed categories but can change over time and across social contexts. One influential example of this perspective is Alfred Kinsey's continuum of sexuality, which challenged the idea that people are either heterosexual or homosexual. Instead, Kinsey argued that sexual attraction exists along a spectrum, with many people experiencing varying degrees of attraction throughout their lives.

Viewing sexuality as a spectrum helps explain why individuals may not fit neatly into a single label or may experience shifts in attraction or identity over time. From a sociological perspective, identity development is shaped not only by personal feelings but also by social interactions, cultural expectations, and historical conditions.

Key takeaway: Sexual attraction exists along a spectrum, with many people experiencing varying degrees of attraction throughout their lives. As people move through the life course, new experiences and social environments can influence how they understand and express their gender and sexual identity.

## LGBTQIA+ and Identities

As understandings of gender and sexuality have expanded, so has the language used to describe them. Sociologists emphasize that identity terms are socially meaningful because they reflect how people understand themselves and how society recognizes differences. However, sexuality and gender terminology are constantly changing and may mean different things to different people.

The acronym LGBTQIA+ is commonly used as an umbrella term. The plus sign (+) acknowledges that not all identities fit neatly into a single label. The following table presents definitions for each term.

## Term Definition

Lesbian This term represents a woman who is emotionally, romantically, or sexually attracted to other women. Women and nonbinary people may use this term to describe themselves.

Gay This term represents a person whose romantic, emotional, or sexual attraction is toward their own gender (most commonly used for men but also by women and nonbinary people).

Bisexual This term represents a person who is emotionally, romantically, or sexually attracted to more than one gender, though not necessarily simultaneously in the same way or to the same degree. It is sometimes used interchangeably with pansexual.

Transgender This is an umbrella term for people whose gender identity and/or expression is different from cultural expectations based on the sex they were assigned at birth. Being transgender does not imply any specific sexual orientation. Therefore, transgender people may identify as straight, gay, lesbian, bisexual, etc.

Queer This is a term people often use to express a spectrum of identities and orientations that are counter to the mainstream. Queer is often used as a catch-all to include many people, including those who do not identify as exclusively straight and/or folks who have nonbinary or gender-expansive identities. This term was previously used as a slur but has been reclaimed by many parts of the LGBTQIA+ movement.

Intersex Intersex people are born with a variety of differences in their sex traits and reproductive anatomy. There is a wide variety of differences among intersex variations, including differences in genitalia, chromosomes, internal sex organs, hormone production, hormone response, and/or secondary sex traits.

Asexual Often called "ace" for short, asexual refers to a complete or partial lack of sexual attraction or lack of interest in sexual activity with others. Asexuality exists on a spectrum, and asexual people may experience no, little, or conditional sexual attraction.

## Key

intersex: describing people who are born with variations in sex traits, such as reproductive anatomy, chromosomes, or hormone patterns, that do not fit typical definitions of male or female

heterosexuality: attraction to individuals of a different gender

homosexuality: attraction to individuals of the same gender; the terms gay and lesbian are commonly used to describe men and women with this sexual orientation, respectively

bisexuality: attraction to individuals of more than one gender

asexuality: experiencing little or no sexual attraction to others, though asexual individuals may still form emotional or romantic relationships

pansexuality: attraction to people regardless of their sex, gender, gender identity, or gender expression

transgender: describing people whose gender identity does not correspond with the sex they were assigned at birth

lesbian: a woman who is emotionally, romantically, or sexually attracted to other women

gay: a person whose romantic, emotional, or sexual attraction is toward their own gender; most commonly used for men but also by women and nonbinary people

queer: a term people often use to express a spectrum of identities and orientations that are counter to the mainstream
