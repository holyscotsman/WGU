## Functionalist Perspective on Deviance

From a functionalist perspective, deviance can be useful in society. Émile Durkheim argued that deviance is a normal and necessary part of society. Because deviance is connected to social facts (shared rules, values, and beliefs), it is a necessary part of society, and it functions by doing the following:

defining boundaries

reinforcing norms

adapting to change

Key takeaway: From a functionalist perspective, deviance is a normal part of society that helps define norms, maintain social order, and promote social change.

## Functionalist Perspective: Robert Merton's

## Strain Theory

Robert Merton's strain theory explains the discrepancy between the goals society tells people to reach (e.g., success, wealth, status) and the structural means for achieving those goals (e.g., education, jobs, hard work). Merton identified five ways people adapt (respond) to this strain:

conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism, or rebellion.

## Bonding Theory

Travis Hirschi introduces social bonding theory, which is based on the theory of social control, derived from the work of Walter Reckless. It starts from the assumption that all people share the same predisposition to engage in deviance and crime, so we do not need to ask what causes these acts. Rather, we should ask what causes people to conform to the norms of their given society.

Hirschi’s Social Bonding theory holds that social control depends on people anticipating the consequences of deviant behavior. That is, people engage in deviant acts when their bonds or ties to society (outer controls) are weak or broken.

There are four main elements that make up an individual’s bond to society:

1. Attachment: our social and emotional ties to each other

2. Commitment: our investment in normative goals and activities

3. Involvement: our participation in conventional activities (time commitment)

4. Belief: accepting the norms, laws, and values of your society

Key takeaway: Hirschi's social bonding theory, which is based on the theory of social control, explains that deviance occurs when a person's connection to society is weak or broken.

## Crime

The conflict perspective explains deviance and crime as outcomes of unequal access to resources and power, which shape how laws are created and enforced. From this perspective, crime is not defined by the harm or morality of a behavior but by whether powerful groups have defined that behavior as illegal or deviant.

From the conflict perspective, groups with greater access to resources, such as wealth, education, and political influence, are more able to shape laws in their favor and are less likely to face punishment, while groups with fewer resources are more likely to be criminalized for their actions.

Key takeaway: From the conflict perspective, deviance and crime are shaped by social inequity due to power differences in society, with laws and punishments often favoring those who have more social and economic power.

## Key

strain theory: a theory explaining deviance as the result of a gap between societal goals and appropriate means

conformity: accepting society's goals and using appropriate means to pursue them

innovation: accepting society's goals but using deviant or illegal means to achieve them

ritualism: rejecting society's goals while continuing to follow accepted rules

retreatism: rejecting both society's goals and the means to achieve them

rebellion: rejecting existing goals and means and replacing them with new ones

social bonding theory: a theory explaining deviance as a result of weak or broken bonds to society

social interaction: a social exchange in which individuals act, communicate, and respond to one another
