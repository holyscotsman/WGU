## Introduction to Social Stratification

Do you ever notice how some groups of people have more or less than you do? Have you noticed that some groups live in fancy houses with gated neighborhoods and attend well-funded high-tech schools, and some groups live in small, crowded neighborhoods and attend schools with outdated textbooks? Or maybe you are not in either group. This is an example of social stratification, which is a system by which society ranks and categorizes groups of people based on access to valued resources such as wealth, income, education, power, and prestige. It is a characteristic of society and not a matter of individual differences. Key takeaway: Social stratification forms a social hierarchy (ranking) within society and influences access to opportunities, which leads to inequality. For example, families with greater wealth and education may have greater access to opportunities, while families with fewer resources face more limitations.

Factors Influencing Placement within the System of Social Stratification There are many factors influencing the placement of individuals and groups within a system of social stratification. Influences include the following three Ps: property, power, and prestige. property wealth income power prestige education occupation status Example: Status as an Influence on Ranking within Society Status is one factor that influences social stratification because it can affect access to resources, opportunities, and influence. There are two types of status: Ascribed status is a social position assigned at birth or involuntarily later in life, based on characteristics like race, sex, family background, or ethnicity. Achieved status is a social position acquired through personal effort, talent, choices, or actions, such as gender, position at work, chess skills, etc. For example, getting married, being a college graduate, and getting a job all represent achieved statuses since these require effort to attain, while being a female is an ascribed status since it is assigned to you at birth.

Property focuses on wealth and income, power refers to the ability to influence decisions and control resources, and prestige relates to education, occupation, and social status. The layout helps illustrate how these three elements contribute to an individual’s position within society.

Key takeaway: Social stratification is the structured ranking of groups in society based on the three Ps: property, power, and prestige. Status, whether ascribed or achieved, is a factor that influences social stratification.

## Systems of Stratification

Many factors influence how individuals and groups are positioned within society. While all societies organize people using systems of stratification, these systems vary in how rigid they are and how much mobility they allow. The following are three systems of stratification used globally.

## Slavery

Slavery is the most extreme form of stratification. In this system, individuals are treated as property and denied basic rights. Social mobility is not possible because status is forced and permanent.

## Caste System

In a caste system, people are born into unequal groups based on their parents' status and remain in these groups for the rest of their lives. Social boundaries are strict, and people are expected to marry

and work within their caste. Mobility is very limited or nonexistent. For example, India is an example of a caste system composed of four tiers called Varna and rooted in the Hindu religious tradition. Historically, the caste system in India operated as a hereditary form of social stratification in which individuals were born into fixed social groups that determined their status, occupation, and access to resources. One's caste shaped nearly every aspect of life, from education and jobs to friendships and marriage, creating a deeply entrenched hierarchy. These divisions were reinforced over generations, making movement between castes nearly impossible. Today, India's legal framework explicitly prohibits caste-based discrimination, yet it still exists, albeit to a much lesser extent.

## Class System

A class system is more mobile. Unlike slavery or caste systems, class systems allow for social mobility, meaning individuals may move up (upward social mobility) or down (downward social mobility) with changes in income, wealth, education, or employment.

Slavery is described as the most rigid system, caste as very rigid, and class as the most flexible. The graphic highlights key characteristics of each system, including how status is assigned and whether social mobility is possible.

Key takeaway: Stratification systems differ in how rigid they are and in the degree of social

mobility they allow. Slavery is the most rigid, then the caste system, and then the class system. Social mobility refers to movement within a system of social stratification and can occur upward or downward, across generations or within a lifetime, and sometimes because of broader societal structural changes.

Social Class and Social Stratification in the United States The United States operates under a class system. Within a class system, individuals are layered into social classes, which refers to a person's rank within the class system. In the United States, the social classes are often categorized relative to their access to the three Ps: power, prestige, and property.

## Social Classes in the United States

Upper class: The upper class is considered the top 1% of the population, and they own one-third of the country's wealth. They are the powerful elite and typically inherit their wealth generationally or from founding large corporations. Middle class: In the United States, the middle class is broken into upper and lower subcategories. Upper-middle-class members tend to hold bachelor's and postgraduate degrees, and consist of professionals with high levels of education, such as CEO's, doctors, and lawyers. Lower-middle-class members hold bachelor's degrees from four-year colleges or associate's degrees from two-year community or technical colleges. Lower class (working class): The lower class, also referred to as the working class, has less formal education and earns smaller incomes compared to the lower-middle class. Key takeaway: Sociologists disagree on the number of social classes in the United States, but a common view is that the United States has five classes: upper, middle, upper-middle, lower-middle, and lower (working class). Further variations exist within the upper and middle classes.

## Class System and Social Mobility

## Downward Mobility

Why do individuals experience downward mobility? Some people move downward because of business setbacks, unemployment, or illness. Dropping out of school, losing a job, or getting a divorce may result in a loss of income or status and, therefore, downward mobility.

## Upward Mobility

Conversely, people can experience upward mobility through educational attainment, career advancement, marriage, or even structural changes in society. Just because upward mobility is possible does not mean that it is easy. Factors such as gender, immigration status, and race are shown to affect the ease of moving upward in social ranking.

## Structural Mobility

When society changes, it can raise or lower the social position of entire groups of people, which is called structural mobility. Structural mobility is a type of social mobility driven by large-scale societal changes rather than individual effort. For example, when the financial crisis happened at the end of 2008, a large number of people lost their retirement funding, and the nation saw an entire group of people's wealth decline.

## Economic Stratification: Wealth and Power

Wealth (the total value of assets a person or family owns, minus its debts and liabilities) is one of the factors that influences social stratification, because it provides long-term advantages. For example, a family with significant wealth may be able to pay for private education, invest in business, or pass on assets to the next generation, strengthening their social position over time.

## Trends in the Distribution of Wealth in the United States

While income inequality has increased significantly since the 1970s, wealth is even more unevenly distributed. The top 1% of Americans hold a growing share of the nation's wealth, while the bottom 90% hold a shrinking share, showing that wealth is highly concentrated among the very rich. Inflation (and its effects) is how the bottom 50%'s wealth can increase over time while simultaneously becoming a smaller percentage of total wealth.

Sociological Perspectives and Theories of Stratification Why do these systems of social stratification exist? Two important sociologists coming from the conflict perspective have developed different theories to explain why stratification exists and how it operates in society.

## Table: Karl Marx and Max Weber Theories of Social Stratification

## Theorist

## Karl Marx

## Theory of Social Stratification

Stratification is based on economic differences between social classes. Stratification creates conflict because the interests of these groups are not equal. Marx believed society is divided between those who own the means of production (the bourgeoisie) and those who are exploited for their labor (the proletariat).

## Max Weber

Stratification is not based only on relationship to the means of production. Weber identified three components of social position that interact to shape social inequality:

- class (economic resources E.g., property) )
- status (social prestige)
- party/power (political influence)
