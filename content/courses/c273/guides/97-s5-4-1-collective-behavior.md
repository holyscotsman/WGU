## Change

When large groups of people begin acting together, their behavior can sometimes lead to major social changes. Sociologists study these patterns through concepts such as social movements and collective behavior, which explain how groups form, organize, and influence society.

## Important Key Terms

Social change is a change in a society created through social movements as well as through external factors like environmental shifts or technological innovations. Social movements are a purposeful, organized group hoping to work toward a common social goal. Collective behavior is a noninstitutionalized activity in which several people voluntarily engage.

Collective Behavior

Sometimes large groups of people begin acting in the same way at the same time, even if they do not know each other. For example, in London in 2009, more than 300 people suddenly began dancing to a Black Eyed Peas song in the middle of a train station. At first, it looked like one person started dancing alone. Then more and more people joined in. Within minutes, the crowd had grown into a coordinated dance performance called a flash mob. After the song ended, participants quickly dispersed, and the station returned to normal. This is an example of collective behavior. Other examples include commuters rushing to catch a train or large groups of teens adopting the same popular hairstyle. In short, collective behavior is group behavior that is not directed or regulated by formal institutions.

Crowds: A Form of Collective Behavior Collective behavior includes many types of group activity that formal institutions do not direct. One of the most visible and immediate forms of collective behavior is the crowd. A crowd is a fairly large number of people in proximity who have a shared goal or focus. Examples include a group of people attending an Ani DiFranco concert, tailgating at a Patriots game, or attending a worship service.

Why Do Crowds Behave Differently from Individuals? In the past, some sociologists believed that crowds were irrational and easily swept up in emotion. Today, sociologists see crowd behavior as more logical. Emergent norm theory explains that when people face new or unusual situations, they create new shared rules, or norms, to guide their behavior. What may look like chaos to outsiders, such as looting during Hurricane Katrina, may be seen by participants as necessary for survival. Leaders or outspoken individuals can influence these new norms, helping the crowd act in organized and purposeful ways.

Mass: A Form of Collective Behavior In addition to crowds, collective groups can also be identified as a mass. A mass is a relatively large number of people with a common interest, though they may not be in close proximity (Lofland 1993). Examples include watch parties across the country for the same television series or show. Both crowds and masses represent forms of collective behavior. They both involve a large number of people who, in some way, are responding to a shared focus or interest.
