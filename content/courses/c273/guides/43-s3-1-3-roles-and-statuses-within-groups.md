## Status in Social Life

Think about how people often ask, "What do you do?" when meeting someone new. That question is not just about curiosity; it reflects how society organizes people based on the positions they occupy. Sociologists study social positions within groups and society, and how they influence expectations of behavior.

## Status, Status Set, and Master Status

Sociologists use the concept of status to explain social positions within groups and society. A status is a social position an individual occupies within a group or society. Examples include employee, parent, student, manager, or neighbor.

Statuses are important because they help groups do the following:

stay organized and predictable

assign expectations and enforce norms

create order in social interactions

Most individuals hold more than one status at the same time. This collection of positions is known as a status set, which includes all the statuses a person occupies. For example, someone may simultaneously hold the statuses of employee, parent, student, and soccer coach. Each status places the individual within different groups, each with its own expectations.

In some situations, one status becomes especially important in shaping how others see an individual. This is known as a master status, which is the status that most strongly influences social identity in a particular context. For example, a person's occupation may function as a master status in professional settings, guiding how others interact with them. Being a graduate of a higher education institution may also be a master status. Master status can also include race and sex, reflecting inequality when this status stands out more than their professional achievements.

Understanding status, status sets, and master status helps you analyze how groups structure social interaction and prepares you to understand how roles enforce norms.

## Roles and Role Expectations

Once a status is established, groups and society attach a set of behaviors, duties, and expectations to that status. Sociologists refer to this as a role. Think about how you behave differently at work, at home, or when you are a student in a class. You are likely to follow different expectations in each role, even without being reminded.

Role expectation is one or more specific behaviors or qualities people expect from someone in that role. For example, the status of a WGU student carries role expectations such as holding regular calls with your program mentor, staying on track with course end dates, and working with your course instructors.

Key takeaway: A role is the behaviors, duties, and expectations that society associates with a particular status. A specific behavior or quality that is expected of someone in a particular role is a role expectation.

## Role Strain and Role Conflict

Two common challenges related to roles are role strain and role conflict. Both concepts highlight the pressure that groups place on individuals to conform to norms.

Role strain occurs when the expectations of a single status become difficult to manage, or even contradict each other, such as a parent trying to find the balance between having fun with their child and being a disciplinarian.

Role conflict occurs when the expectations of two or more statuses clash, such as when work responsibilities interfere with family responsibilities.

Key takeaway: Role strain occurs when the role expectations within one status become too difficult to manage. Role conflict occurs when the role expectations of two different statuses clash.

## Key

status: a social position an individual occupies within a group or society

role: the behaviors, duties, and expectations associated with a particular status

role expectations: the specific norms and behaviors a group expects to be completed by someone occupying a particular status; can differ geographically and from one context to another

role strain: tension that arises when the demands of a single status are difficult to meet role conflict: tension that occurs when the expectations of two or more statuses conflict with one another
