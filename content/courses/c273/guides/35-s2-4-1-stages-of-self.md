## Introduction to Stages of Self

How do individuals go from being newborns to being humans with their own self-identity? Sociologist George Herbert Mead believed that there are stages of development that all people go through. Mead believed that the self is a person's sense of identity and does not exist at birth; instead, it develops through social interactions over time.

According to Mead's theory of self-development, individuals learn who they are by interacting with others and gradually learning to see themselves from others' perspectives. This process unfolds through a series of stages, based loosely on the child's age (though there will always be exceptions and variations). According to Mead's theory of self-development, there are three stages:

1. preparatory stage

2. play stage

3. game stage

## The Preparatory Stage

The first stage of self-development is the preparatory stage. During the preparatory stage, individuals mimic the behaviors, gestures, and sounds of people with whom they regularly interact, such as parents and caregivers. For example, a child may wave goodbye, smile, or laugh simply because they observe others doing so. At this point, children do not have the ability to imagine how others see them, nor can they take the role of another person. Their actions are a simple repetition of observed behavior.

## The Beginning of Socialization

This stage represents the beginning of socialization:

Imitation allows the individual to start engaging with the social world.

Individuals lack self-awareness and perspective-taking.

Individuals have not yet developed a true sense of self.

Key takeaway: In the preparatory stage, children begin socialization by imitating the behaviors of others, but they do not yet understand perspectives or possess a developed sense of self.

## The Play Stage

The play stage follows the preparatory stage and occurs when individuals begin role-taking, meaning they practice becoming members of society by pretending to be someone else. However, at this stage, children can only take on one role at a time and do not yet understand how roles fit together within a larger social system. For example, a child may pretend to be a superhero, a princess, a teacher, or a nurse. They might also try on a parent's point of view by acting out "grown-up" behavior, like playing dress-up and acting out the mom role or talking on a toy telephone the way they see adults do.

Mead referred to this stage as the play stage because they learn social roles by pretending to be others in informal, imaginative ways rather than by following structured rules.

Key takeaway: In the play stage, children move further in developing the self by engaging in role-taking, learning expected behaviors one role at a time, but they do not yet understand shared rules or how multiple roles fit together.

## The Game Stage

In the game stage, individuals develop a more advanced understanding of social interaction. During this stage, they learn to consider several roles at the same time, rules, coordination, and shared expectations. Unlike the play stage, where they focus on only one role, the game stage requires children to understand multiple roles.

For example, at this stage they are able to participate in organized activities with structured rules, such as team sports or board games, where they must understand their own role, the roles of others, and the rules that govern everyone's behavior. Successfully participating requires children to see how individual actions affect the group.

## Generalized Other

This stage is also when children develop an understanding of the generalized other, which refers to the community expectations that individuals follow social rules. Children learn that there are people "out there" who expect them to follow the norms of society, and this understanding allows them to imagine how they are viewed by one or many others. At this point, from a sociological perspective, the individual has developed a self.

Key takeaway: In the game stage, individuals develop a full sense of self by understanding multiple roles, shared rules, and the expectations of the broader society, allowing them to see themselves from the perspective of the generalized other.

## The "I" and the "Me": Self-Consciousness

According to Mead, a person achieves self-awareness when they begin to understand the difference between the "I" and the "Me."

The "I" The "I" is described as the spontaneous and creative part of the self. It allows for creativity, choice, and unpredictability and represents a person's individual response to social expectations.

The "Me" The "Me" represents the socialized self, meaning the part of the self that is shaped by societal expectations, norms, and rules. The "Me" reflects what individuals learn through interaction with others and their awareness of the generalized other, or the common behavioral expectations of general society.

Example of the "I" and the "Me" If someone cuts you off in traffic, your "I" may want to scream or drive aggressively, but your "Me" tells you that is not appropriate or desired behavior.

Key takeaway: Together, the "I" and the "Me" explain how people are shaped by society while still acting as independent individuals. The "I" is the spontaneous and creative part of the self, and the "Me" represents the socialized self.

## Key

preparatory stage: the earliest stage of the development of the self, in which children imitate the behaviors of others without understanding their meaning or intentions play stage: a stage of self-development in which children begin to take the roles of specific others, such as parents or teachers, and understand behavior from those individual perspectives role-taking: the process of assuming the perspective of another person to understand their thoughts, feelings, and expectations

game stage: a stage of self-development in which individuals understand and respond to the roles and expectations of multiple others at the same time

generalized other: the internalized sense of the shared expectations, norms, and values of the larger society that guides behavior

the "I": the spontaneous, creative, and impulsive part of the self that represents personal responses to social situations

the "Me": the socialized part of the self that reflects internalized norms, rules, and expectations learned through interaction with others
