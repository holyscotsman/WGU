# S3 3.1 Deviance and Crime

Why do societies treat some rule-breaking behaviors as minor issues while punishing others as crimes?

What Is Deviance? Think about everyday situations where someone breaks a rule, such as talking during a movie, cutting in line, or wearing inappropriate clothing to a formal event. These actions may draw negative reactions, but they are not always illegal. Sociologists are interested in why certain behaviors draw disapproval and how societies decide what counts as unacceptable behavior. In each of these situations, the problem is not the action itself but the fact that it violates shared norms about how people are supposed to behave. Sociologists refer to these violations as deviance, which are behaviors, beliefs, or characteristics that violate social norms. (Recall that social norms are normal, shared, and accepted patterns of behavior in your society.)

When and Why Is Behavior Deviant?

When is behavior deviant? A behavior becomes deviant only when a group or society decides that it violates shared norms for behavior. Factors Explaining Deviant Behavior The following factors help explain why the same behavior may be acceptable in some situations but considered deviant in others. Culture: Different societies have different rules. What is normal in one culture may be unacceptable in another. Time period: Social rules change. Behaviors once criticized may later become normal (and vice versa). Context: Behavior that fits one setting may violate expectations in another, and this may change geographically. Audience: Who is present matters. People react differently depending on whether friends, authority figures, or strangers are watching. Power: Some people and institutions have more authority to decide what counts as deviant and how it is punished.

Is Deviance Always Bad? Sociologists recognize that deviance is not always harmful or morally wrong. In some cases, breaking social norms can challenge unfair rules or outdated expectations and draw attention to social problems.

## Examples of Positive Outcomes of Deviance

Behaviors such as protesting unjust laws, reporting wrongdoing, or refusing to participate in discrimination may violate existing norms. Still, they can also prompt societies to reconsider what is acceptable and necessary. In this way, deviance can contribute to social change by encouraging reflection and improvement rather than simply disrupting social order.

Deviance can also violate dangerous social norms and affect society positively. For example, driving the speed limit is the law, but in most cities in the United States, it is not the norm. Driving the speed limit despite negative reactions from other drivers can show your focus on safety rather than conformity.

Deviance versus Criminal Behavior People break rules in many ways, but not all rule-breaking is treated the same. Some actions may result in disapproval or social pressure (deviance), while others lead to arrest or legal punishment (a crime). A crime is defined as an action that violates a formal law. Laws are norms that are formally written, enforced by the government, and backed by legal consequences.

## Crime as a Social Concept

Because societies create laws, what counts as a crime can vary across places and times. Behaviors that are illegal in one society may be legal in another, and laws can change as societal values change. This reinforces the sociological idea that crime, like deviance, is socially defined rather than universal. Why Do Sociologists Study Crime? Sociologists study crime not only to understand why laws are broken but also to examine how laws reflect societal values, power, and priorities. The distinction between deviance and crime helps clarify why some behaviors are handled informally through social pressure, while others involve formal punishment through the legal system.

## Sanctions as Social Control: How Deviance

## Is Regulated

Once societies define what behaviors are acceptable and unacceptable, they must find ways to encourage conformity and respond to deviance. Sociologists refer to these efforts as social control—the processes through which groups regulate behavior and maintain social order. One of the main tools of social control is the use of sanctions, which are rewards and punishments that reinforce social norms.

## Positive and Negative Sanctions

When people follow norms, positive sanctions can signal approval. When norms are violated, negative sanctions communicate that a behavior is unacceptable and may discourage

future violations.

This infographic compares positive and negative social sanctions. The left side, labeled Positive, lists examples such as praise, privileges, recognition, and promotion, represented with check marks. The right side, labeled Negative, lists examples such as embarrassment, exclusion, detention or suspension, and fines or jail time, represented with X marks.

## Formal and Informal Sanctions

Sanctions also differ based on whether they are enforced socially (informal sanctions) or institutionally (formal sanctions). Informal sanctions are reactions that come from everyday social interactions. These sanctions arise from face-to-face social interactions and may include reactions from peers, family members, or the community, such as gossip, disapproval, or social exclusion. Formal sanctions are enforced by institutions such as schools, workplaces, courts, or governments. These sanctions involve official consequences tied to written rules or laws, such as suspension, termination, fines, or incarceration.

This infographic contrasts informal and formal sanctions in society. The left column, titled Informal Sanctions, shows examples of social responses from friends, classmates, coworkers, and family members, such as disapproval, gossip, or exclusion. Key takeaway: Sanctions are the rewards and punishments societies use to enforce norms. They can be positive or negative and formal or informal, and they play a key role in encouraging conformity and responding to deviant behavior.

Key Terms deviance: behaviors, beliefs, or characteristics that violate social norms norms: shared expectations about how people are supposed to think or behave socially constructed: the idea that meanings, rules, or behaviors are defined by society rather than being naturally fixed crime: an action that violates a formal law and is punishable by the state law: a formally written rule that is enforced by government authority social control: the processes through which societies regulate behavior and maintain social order sanctions: rewards or punishments used to encourage conformity to social norms positive sanctions: rewards that reinforce acceptable or approved behavior negative sanctions: punishments that discourage norm violations informal sanctions: social reactions from individuals or groups that enforce norms without official authority formal sanctions: official consequences enforced by institutions such as schools, workplaces, or the legal system power: the ability of individuals or groups to influence rules, norms, and how deviance is defined and punished, even when resisted
