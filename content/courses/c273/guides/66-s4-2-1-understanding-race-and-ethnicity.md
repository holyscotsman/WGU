## Race and Social Stratification

Social stratification becomes easier to understand when examined through specific social categories that societies use to organize people. One of the most common and visible of these social categories is race. Race is a categorization of humankind based on shared physical or cultural traits that can vary from one society to another and from one historical moment to another.

"Race Is a Social Construct: QED with Dr. B" In the past, many people incorrectly believed race was biological because physical differences were thought to reflect natural group divisions. Today, sociologists and scientists recognize that race is socially constructed, meaning societies decide which traits matter and how people are grouped. Throughout history, these racial categories have often been used to differentiate people and create social hierarchies that give some groups more power and advantage than others. Although socially constructed, race has real consequences that shape social status, economic opportunities, and health outcomes.

## Fluidity of Race

Because race is socially constructed, racial categories can change across time and place. This concept is referred to as fluidity of race. In the United States, racial categories have changed repeatedly. For example, groups such as Irish, Italian, and Eastern European immigrants were not considered White when they first arrived in the United States. Over time, however, as the political climate changed and social definitions shifted, these groups were classified as White. These changes reflect shifting social power dynamics and help us understand race as a social construction rather than changes in human biology. The traits used to classify people as belonging to a particular race are not universal and differ across countries. Different societies emphasize different physical or social characteristics when creating racial categories, and those choices can shift as social conditions change. For example, a person considered Black in the United States may be classified differently in Brazil, where race is often understood along a continuum of skin tones rather than a small number of fixed categories. This means the same individual can be placed into different racial categories depending on the society. Key takeaway: Racial categories are fluid; they change across time and place as societies define and redefine how people are grouped.

## Ethnicity

Race is one way societies organize people into social categories. Societies also organize people using shared cultural characteristics, which are captured by the concept of ethnicity. Ethnicity is the shared culture, practices, norms, values, and beliefs of a group that might include language, religion, and traditions, among other commonalities. Examples of ethnic groups include Korean, Cherokee, Slavic, Armenian, Mongolian, etc.

## Ethnicity and Race Intertwined

Since race and ethnicity are both socially constructed, their meanings have changed over time. And, as with race, individuals may be identified or self-identify with ethnicities in complex, even contradictory, ways. For example, ethnic groups such as Irish, Italian American, Russian, Jewish, and Serbian might all be groups whose members are predominantly included in the White racial category. Key takeaway: Ethnicity is a social category based on shared culture and national origin, and ethnic groups form through shared experiences such as migration, settlement, and common historical events.

## Minority and Dominant Groups

Race and ethnicity describe how societies categorize people, but sociologists also examine how those categories are positioned within social structure and may produce inequality for some and advantages for others. This is where the concept of minority groups and dominant groups becomes important. A minority group is a group that experiences discrimination and unequal treatment within a society. Importantly, minority status is not determined by numerical size. Instead, sociologists emphasize power and access to resources. For example, women are considered a minority group in the United States despite making up roughly half of the population. A dominant group is the group that holds greater power, privilege, and social advantage within a stratification system. Membership in the dominant group is often treated as normal or unmarked, while minority group membership is more likely to be noticed, labeled, or regulated. Key takeaway: Minority and dominant groups are defined by power and unequal treatment, not by how many people belong to each group.
