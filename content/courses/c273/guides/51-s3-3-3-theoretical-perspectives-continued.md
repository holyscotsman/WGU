Symbolic Interactionist Perspective:

- Differential Association Theory

In the early 1900s, sociologist Edwin Sutherland sought to understand how deviant behavior developed among people. His conclusions established differential association theory, which suggested that individuals learn deviant behavior from those close to them who provide models of and opportunities for deviance. According to Sutherland, deviance is less a personal choice and more a result of differential socialization processes. For example, a young person whose friends are sexually active is more likely to view sexual activity as acceptable.

## Perspective: Labeling Theory

How do social reactions turn a behavior into deviance that shapes someone's identity? To answer this question, sociologists use labeling theory, a theory stemming from the symbolic interactionist perspective. Labeling theory explains that deviance can move from being defined by others through social interaction without affecting an individual's self-image long term (primary deviance) to being internalized by individuals as part of their self-identity (secondary deviance).

## Label Theory Example: Medicalization of Deviance

Labeling theory shows how behaviors become defined as deviant through social reactions and the labels applied by others. Labels can include calling a child a troublemaker or an ex-convict a felon. In the latter, this label is considered a stigma, a negative label that can devalue a person's social identity. In some cases, however, deviant behavior is not labeled as immoral or criminal but instead is redefined as a medical issue. This shift in how deviance is labeled is referred to as the medicalization of deviance.
