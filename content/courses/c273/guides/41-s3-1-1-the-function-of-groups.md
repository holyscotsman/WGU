## Groups and Nongroups

Imagine you are part of a study group that meets every week. Members check in, share notes, divide tasks, and expect each other to show up prepared. Over time, the group develops shared routines and a sense of "us." This is the kind of social connection sociologists have in mind when they use the term group.

From a sociological perspective, a group is two or more people who interact regularly and share a sense of identity or belonging. Through groups, people learn social norms, build relationships, and coordinate behavior. In other words, groups are one of the main ways society shapes individual behavior and creates predictable patterns of interaction.

When is a Group not a Group?

This question is not a riddle! Imagine standing in line at a grocery store during a busy evening. Other people surround you, but no one is talking, coordinating, or sharing a sense of belonging. Even though many people are present, a sociologist would not consider this a group.

A nongroup is more than one person who may be in the same place at the same time, share certain characteristics, but do not necessarily share an identity, interact, or feel tied to each other in any way. Sociologists distinguish groups from nongroups because they want to separate meaningful social relationships from just a collection of people.

Sociologists identify two types of nongroups based on why people are together.

An aggregate is a collection of people who are in the same place at the same time but do not interact or share a sense of identity. For example, people at a grocery store form an aggregate at the same time.

A category is a group of people who share a characteristic but do not necessarily interact with one another and do not need to be in the same proximity. Examples include people who wear glasses, people of the same age range, or individuals who share a similar occupation.

Key takeaway: Groups are defined by regular interaction and shared identity, and they play an important role in shaping social behavior, relationships, and social organization. Recognizing this difference helps sociologists understand how social behavior and relationships are formed.

## Types of Groups (Primary and Secondary)

Once sociologists identify what counts as a group and distinguish groups from nongroups, they further classify groups based on the type of relationships and level of social connection involved.

## Primary Groups

Think about the people you turn to for support during a stressful time, such as family members or close friends. These relationships tend to be personal, emotionally meaningful, and long-lasting. Sociologists refer to these close social connections as primary groups. Primary groups are small groups characterized by strong emotional ties, frequent interaction, and a shared sense of belonging.

Primary groups are powerful because they shape the following:

self-concept (how you see yourself)

values and beliefs

enduring emotional connections

early socialization

## Secondary Groups

Now consider a group that has completed a task, such as a workplace team, college class, or support group. Members interact regularly, but the relationships are focused on achieving a goal rather than building emotional bonds. Sociologists call these secondary groups. Secondary groups are larger, temporary, and more impersonal groups organized around a specific purpose or activity.

Secondary groups help society function by organizing people around the following:

efficiency

productivity

shared tasks and goals

emotional/wellness goal support

## Comparing Primary and Secondary Groups

The following table compares the features of primary and secondary groups.

## Feature Primary Group Secondary Group

## Size Small Larger

## Relationship Type Close, personal Formal, role-based

## Duration Long-term Temporary or ongoing

## Level of Social High Low to moderate

## Connection

Function Belonging/support Functional/accomplishing a goal

Example Best friends Employees of an organization

Key takeaway: Primary groups are defined by close, emotional relationships, while secondary groups are organized around goals and tasks, and both play important roles in society. Learning the difference helps us understand our own relationships better. It shows us why peer pressure feels more intense coming from friends than coworkers, or why family arguments feel different from disagreements with coworkers.

## Key

group: two or more people who interact regularly and share a sense of identity or belonging

nongroup: a collection of people that lacks regular interaction and shared identity

aggregate: people in the same proximity at the same time but do not interact or form social ties

category: people who share a common characteristic but may never interact with one another and who are not necessarily in the same place

primary group: a small, close-knit group with strong emotional ties and long-lasting relationships, such as family or close friends

secondary group: a larger, temporary, more impersonal group organized around a specific goal or task, such as a workplace or class
