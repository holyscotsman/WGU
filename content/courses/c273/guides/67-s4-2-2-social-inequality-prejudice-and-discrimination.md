## Social Inequality and W. E. B. Du Bois

Social stratification ranks groups in society, and these rankings lead to social inequality. Social inequality is the unequal distribution of resources, opportunities, and rewards across social groups. It affects groups rather than isolated individuals and tends to persist over time.

## W. E. B. Du Bois

One of the earliest sociologists to study social inequality related to race was W. E. B. Du Bois. He proposed the following: Racial inequality cannot be explained by biology or individual behavior. Racial inequality brought about the double consciousness, the idea that being an American and being a Black person were two separate identities, defining the lived experiences of Black people. Social institutions such as education, employment, and politics shape unequal outcomes experienced by racial groups. Social inequality should be understood as a structural issue rather than an individual one.

Key takeaway: W. E. B. Du Bois showed that racial inequality is produced by social structures and institutions, not by biology or individual behavior.

Social Inequality: Stereotypes and Prejudice Stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination are closely connected concepts that shape how individuals and groups perceive and treat one another. Together, they influence social relationships, reinforce inequality, and help maintain systems of power and inequality within society.

## Stereotypes

Stereotypes are oversimplified generalizations about groups of people. Stereotypes can be based on race, ethnicity, age, gender, sexual orientation, or almost any characteristic. They may be positive in intention (usually about one's own group) but are often negative (usually toward another group). For example, women being told they are too emotional to lead or people of Asian descent being told they should be good at math.

## Prejudice

A story: Imagine two applicants submitting identical job applications for the same position. Both have similar education, experience, and references, but one checks a box indicating a prior conviction. Research shows that an applicant is far less likely to receive a callback, even when fully qualified. Even when individuals with a criminal conviction have fulfilled their debts to society and rehabilitated themselves, the shadow of a conviction can hang over them for the rest of their lives, limiting their opportunities to secure a job, an apartment, or a loan. The story provides an example of prejudice that exists in our society today. Prejudice refers to the beliefs, thoughts, feelings, and attitudes toward a group, typically based on stereotypes. A prejudice is not tested against reality and can be based on limited or no personal experience. For example, stereotypes about certain racial or ethnic groups may lead people to form negative expectations about individuals they have never met. Prejudice can even result in moral double standards when judging people or actions of in-groups versus out-groups.

## Discrimination

While prejudice describes biased thinking, discrimination refers to actions taken against individuals or groups based on prejudice. Discrimination can be based on race, ethnicity, age, religion, health, disability status, and other categories. For example, discrimination based on race or ethnicity can take many forms, from biased hiring systems to unfair housing practices such as redlining. Redlining is a discriminatory practice of denying financial services, such as mortgages and insurance, to residents of specific, often minorityheavy neighborhoods. Historical examples in the United States include exclusionary hiring practices such as "No Irish Need Apply" signs and segregation under Jim Crow laws.

## Individual versus Institutional Discrimination

Discrimination can occur at different levels. Individual discrimination occurs when one person treats another unfairly based on group membership. Institutional discrimination occurs when policies or systems within social institutions create disadvantages for certain groups, such as discriminatory housing practices like redlining.

## Explanations of Prejudice

How do ideas and feelings about social groups influence how people are treated? Sociologists study prejudice to understand how attitudes toward social groups develop and persist. Prejudice is a negative attitude toward an entire category of people; often, but not limited to, ethnic or racial minorities.

Conflict Perspective on Prejudice: The conflict perspective explains prejudice as a result of unequal power and competition for resources within society. This view suggests that dominant groups may promote negative beliefs about other groups to justify unequal treatment, such as lower wages or limited opportunities.

## Functionalist Perspective: Culture Theory of Prejudice

## Culture Theory of Prejudice

Key Idea: Prejudiced beliefs are learned through socialization rather than being innate. Why Prejudice Develops: Prejudice develops as individuals learn beliefs and attitudes from family, media, institutions, and cultural norms. How It Persists: Prejudice persists when these beliefs are reinforced and passed down across generations.

## Perspective: Primarily functionalist perspective

## Conflict Perspective: Scapegoat Theory of Prejudice

## Scapegoat Theory of Prejudice

Key Idea: Prejudice can arise when people redirect frustration and blame toward less powerful groups. Why Prejudice Develops: Prejudice develops when the hardship or social stress is displaced onto a group with less power. How It Persists: Prejudice persists as long as underlying frustrations and social problems remain unresolved.

## Perspective: Primarily conflict perspective

## Conflict Perspective: Intersectionality

## Intersectionality

Intersectionality is not a theory of prejudice. Rather, it is a framework for understanding prejudice. Key Idea: Multiple social identities (such as race, gender, and class) overlap and shape experiences of inequality. Why Prejudice Develops: Prejudice develops in different forms depending on how social categories intersect. How It Persists: Prejudice persists in varied ways because overlapping identities influence how individuals experience discrimination and privilege.

## Perspective: Conflict perspective

## Symbolic Interactionist Perspective: Salience Principle

## Salience Principle

The salience principle can be used to explain prejudice, but it is not itself a theory of prejudice. Key Idea: Prejudice arises from cognitive attention to standout traits rather than deeper individual understanding. When certain characteristics are more noticeable, they are given disproportionate importance in forming impressions. Why Prejudice Develops: Prejudice develops because people naturally attend to salient (highly visible or distinctive) traits, using them as shortcuts to categorize others. How It Persists: Prejudice persists as these salient traits continue to dominate perception, reinforcing stereotypes and guiding biased interpretations of behavior.
