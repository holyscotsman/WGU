# S1 1.2 Foundational Concepts in Sociology

## Initial Reflection

Recall that the sociological perspective involves looking beyond individual experiences to understand the larger social patterns and structures that shape people's lives. Sociologists study societies, identify patterns across groups, and examine how institutions like the economy, education, and family influence behavior.

How do sociologists actually study patterns in society?

## Learning Activity (continued)

When you first hear stories like Bob's and Yuriko's, it is natural to focus on individual characteristics like motivation, personality, decision-making, or effort. But sociologists pause and ask a different question: What larger patterns might be influencing these individual experiences? Now, revisit these stories and widen your lens by examining these situations through a sociologist's viewpoint.

## Story 1 Revisited

Take another look at Bob's situation from a sociologist's viewpoint. Bob is not the only middle-aged worker struggling to find a job. He lives in a region where the industry he spent his entire career in has moved away. Companies have relocated, and the jobs that once supported whole communities simply are not there anymore. The new jobs require skills he was never trained for.

Employers in the area also assume that men at Bob's age will demand higher wages, need more benefits, or expect raises more quickly than younger employees. Some even worry that they will not adapt to new technologies fast enough and thus prefer to hire younger candidates. And even when Bob considers taking lower-paying jobs outside his field, he faces another barrier: stigma. Some of these positions (e.g., movie theater usher, barista) are seen as after-school gigs, not jobs for middle- aged men. That stigma makes Bob reluctant to apply.

## Story 2 Revisited

Take another look at Yuriko's situation from a sociologist's viewpoint. Yuriko is not the only parent struggling with school pickup. Across the country, school schedules end hours before most full-time jobs, creating a widespread mismatch that working parents cannot easily solve. For Yuriko, the challenge is even greater. The jobs near her home do not pay enough to support her family, so she works on the other side of the city. Her long commute, combined with inflexible work hours, makes arriving right after her son gets out of school nearly impossible.

When she moved to California, Yuriko assumed schools would provide school bus transportation like they did in her home state of Pennsylvania. Instead, she discovered that most California districts offer little or no busing for students, leaving parents to manage transportation on their own. Yuriko's struggle is not just about her time management. It reflects broader structural issues: economic segregation, long commutes, limited school transportation, and schedules built around outdated assumptions about family life.

## Feedback

At first glance, Bob and Yuriko's struggles may have seemed like personal problems—issues of effort, decision-making, or individual traits. But when you widen the lens, you begin to see how their experiences connect to broader social patterns such as economic restructuring, workplace expectations, transportation systems, and school policies.

This does not mean that individual choices or responsibilities do not matter. Rather, it means that those choices are made within a social context that shapes the opportunities and constraints people face. A complete explanation considers both individual factors and the broader social forces influencing them.

## How Sociologists Study Society

The shift in sociologists' viewpoint from a personal problem to a broader social context is referred to as the sociological imagination. The sociological imagination is the ability to connect personal experiences to broader social structures and historical forces (including culture). Sociologist C. Wright Mills introduced this idea to help people see how their lives are influenced by society, history, and culture. This way of thinking encourages sociologists to ask not just "What is happening to the individual?" but "What is happening in society that helps explain this?" When sociologists ask these questions, they can begin to distinguish between personal troubles, such as an individual being unemployed, and public issues, such as the percentage of the population who may be unemployed.

When studying situations like Bob's and Yuriko's, sociologists look beyond individual experiences to identify broader social patterns. While each person's story feels unique, sociologists ask whether similar outcomes appear among others living in the same society and facing similar social pressures. If many middle-aged workers in a region are unemployed after industries relocate, or if many working parents struggle with school pickup times due to work schedules, those individual experiences reflect larger social trends. This practice is called seeing the "general in the particular." It means recognizing general patterns in what initially appear to be individual outcomes.

Instructor's note: As you continue in this course, try using your sociological imagination to place the everyday experiences you learn about within a broader social context. And by seeing the general in the particular, you may be able to recognize how individual outcomes are shaped by the wider patterns and structures of society.

What Do Sociologists Study?

1.0/2.0 points (ungraded) Sociologists try to understand the forces that create the patterns in society—what sociologists refer to as social facts. Social facts are the laws, morals, values, customs, and cultural rules that shape social life and influence behavior. Social facts exist outside the individual, but they still affect everyday decisions. For example, most people stop at a red light even when no police officer is nearby because laws and shared expectations (social facts) shape behavior. In addition, school attendance requirements, workplace policies, and legal responsibilities also guide what people do each day, even when individuals do not consciously think about them.

Which statement from Bob's story illustrates a social fact?

Bob plays video games and comments online.

Bob searches for jobs and applies sometimes.

Bob feels down and blames himself.

Jobs in Bob's industry moved away.

## Answer

Incorrect: This is an individual action, not an external social reality that constrains or shapes behavior across many individuals.

## Social Institutions

Understanding social facts naturally leads to a deeper question for sociologists: Which parts of society produce and reinforce these shared rules and expectations? The answer lies in the concept of social institutions. Social institutions are social structures with organized patterns of beliefs and behaviors that meet important needs in society and help people work and live together. In this course, you will be examining social institutions such as family, education, government, religion, healthcare, and the economy.
