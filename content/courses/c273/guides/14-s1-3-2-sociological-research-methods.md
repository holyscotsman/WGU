## Purpose

Sociological research can have different purposes. Some studies describe what is happening in society. Others explain why it is happening.

## Descriptive Research

Descriptive research focuses on what is happening. For example, a sociologist might study how often people use social media, how many students work while in college, or differences in access to healthcare. It answers questions such as the following:

- What is happening?
- How common is this behavior?

Quantitative and Qualitative Research:

- Based on Data and Methods

Sociologists also choose research methods based on the type of data they collect. The two main approaches are quantitative research and qualitative research.

## Quantitative Research

Quantitative research uses numerical data that can be measured and analyzed statistically. It is useful for studying large groups and identifying broad patterns. For example, a sociologist might measure how many people have access to healthcare, how many students work while attending college, or whether tutoring improves grades.

## Qualitative Research

Qualitative research uses non-numerical data, such as interviews, observations, and written responses. It is useful for understanding people's experiences and perspectives. For example, a sociologist might interview students about how working affects their school experience or observe how families respond to stress. ## Choosing the Type of Data

Once sociologists develop a research question, they must decide how they will collect data to answer it. Data is information used to understand social behavior and patterns. Researchers must decide whether they will use primary data or secondary data. The key difference is who collected the data. Primary data is data collected directly by the researcher or research team. Secondary data is data that already exists and was collected by others outside of the current research.

## Primary Data Collection Methods

Sociologists collect primary data using the following methods, each with advantages and disadvantages:

- surveys
- experiments
- field research
- participant observation
- ethnography
- case studies
- content analysis

## Method: Survey

Surveys collect information through questionnaires or interviews. Advantage: They have the ability to collect data from large numbers of people. Disadvantage: People may not answer honestly or provide much detail about a topic.

## Method: Experiments

Experiments test cause and effect under controlled conditions. Advantage: They are replicable by other researchers and can prove cause and effect. Disadvantage: Not all research questions are suitable for this type of study.

## Method: Field Research

Field research takes place in natural settings. Advantage:

- gives in-depth information
- Disadvantage:

may lack generalizability (ability to generalize to the large population) as it relies on context

## Method: Participant Observation

Participant observation involves the researcher joining a group while observing it. Advantage: You can gain an insider's perspective as a researcher. Disadvantage: This can be an ethical concern, since the researcher can lose objectivity.

## Method: Ethnography

Ethnography involves long-term immersion in a group or culture. Advantage:

- extremely rich and detailed data
- Disadvantages:

can be time-consuming, subject to interpretation by the researcher, and lack generalizability to a larger population

## Method: Case Study

A case study focuses on one individual, group, or event. Advantage:

- useful for unique cases and building future research questions
- Disadvantages:
- limited generalizability to a larger population

## Method: Content Analysis

Content analysis examines media or documents for patterns and themes. Advantage:

- an unobtrusive way to collect data
- Disadvantages:
- can be time-consuming; content is subject to interpretation

## Method: Secondary Data Analysis

Secondary data analysis uses data that already exists. For example, census data can be used to examine how education levels differ across regions or how income inequality has changed over time. Advantage:

- saves time and money; access to extensive data collections
- Disadvantage:

may not be an existing data source that matches specifically what you need

## Key Takeaway

Sociologists choose research methods based on their research question. Each method has strengths and limitations. Understanding the pros and cons helps researchers choose the best method. ## Challenges with Data Collection Methods

Even when sociologists follow the scientific method carefully, research can still have limitations. For example, some studies may use small samples that make it harder to apply findings to larger groups, and field research can make it difficult to separate cause and effect because so many factors are happening at once. Sociologists may also face data issues such as missing information, inaccurate records, or existing data that does not measure exactly what they need. Each research method also has specific pros and cons associated with it. Recognizing these challenges helps researchers interpret results carefully and explain what conclusions can and cannot be drawn.

Key Terms descriptive research: research that describes social patterns or behaviors without explaining why they occur explanatory research: research that seeks to explain social phenomena and why we see certain social patterns qualitative research: research that uses non-numerical data to understand social meanings and experiences quantitative research: research that uses numerical data to measure and analyze social phenomena data: information collected to answer a research question primary data: data collected directly by a researcher for a specific study secondary data: data that already exists and was collected by others surveys: a research method that collects data by asking people a series of questions

field research: a research method that collects data in natural social settings participant observation: a field research method in which the researcher participates in a group while observing behavior ethnography: a field research method involving long-term immersion in a social community case study: an in-depth analysis of a single individual, group, or event experiments: a research method that tests cause-and-effect relationships by manipulating variables secondary data analysis: a research method that analyzes data previously collected by other researchers or organizations content analysis: a research method that analyzes written, visual, or audio materials to identify patterns, themes, meanings, or representations
