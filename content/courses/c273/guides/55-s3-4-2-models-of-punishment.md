## The U.S. Criminal Justice System

A criminal justice system is an organization that exists to enforce a legal code. There are three pillars of the U.S. criminal justice system: the police, the courts, and the correctional system.

The following illustration describes each pillar of the U.S. criminal justice system.

This infographic outlines three components of the criminal justice system: police, courts, and the correctional system. Each section includes an icon and a brief explanation of its role. Police are responsible for enforcing laws and maintaining public order. Courts interpret laws and make legal decisions. The correctional system supervises individuals who have been arrested, convicted, or are awaiting trial.

## The Correctional System

When a person is found guilty of a crime, one possible outcome is confinement in a jail or prison. This is referred to as incarceration.

Prisons are facilities built for individuals serving sentences longer than one year. They are typically large institutions operated by either the state or federal governments.

Jails provide short-term confinement. People in jail may be awaiting trial, awaiting sentencing, or serving sentences of less than one year. Jails are usually managed at the local level.

## Probation and Parole

Not everyone is incarcerated. Some individuals are sentenced to probation, which allows them to remain in the community under supervision. Parole occurs when a person is released early from prison but continues to serve the remainder of the sentence under supervision in the community.

## Incarceration Rates

Beyond individual punishment, sociologists also examine how many people are incarcerated and how this changes over time. Incarceration rates help reveal how heavily a society relies on confinement as a response to crime. At the end of 2018, approximately 2.3 million people were incarcerated in the United States; these include people who are in state and federal prisons as well as those in local jails or related facilities.

## The Rise and Decline of Incarceration

The use of incarceration has changed over time. The U.S. incarceration rate has grown considerably in the last hundred years, but it has begun to decline in the past decade.

## Sociological Perspectives of Incarceration

## Perspective Explanation

Functionalist Perspective From the functionalist perspective, prisons help maintain social order, stability, and equilibrium. They remove individuals who violate society's rules, discourage others from committing crimes through deterrence, and provide rehabilitation so prisoners can return to society as productive members.

Conflict Perspective This perspective argues that prisons reflect and reinforce social inequality. People from lower-income groups and minority communities are more likely to be incarcerated due to over-policing and laws that criminalize behaviors more common among disadvantaged groups. Prisons can mask larger social problems like poverty by framing them as individual failures.

Symbolic Interactionist This perspective focuses on how prison affects identity Perspective and social interactions. Being labeled a "criminal" creates stigma that can make it difficult to find jobs, housing, and supportive relationships. This negative label can reinforce a criminal identity and increase the likelihood of re- offending.

## Key

criminal justice system: the system of institutions—including police, courts, and corrections—that enforce laws and respond to crime

court: a legal institution where judges and sometimes juries interpret laws and decide the outcomes of cases

corrections system: the part of the criminal justice system that supervises people who have been convicted of crimes through incarceration, probation, or parole

prisons: secure facilities that hold people convicted of serious crimes, usually for longer sentences

jails: local facilities that hold people awaiting trial or serving short sentences

probation: a court-ordered period of supervision in the community instead of serving time in jail or prison

parole: the conditional release of a prisoner before completing a full sentence, with supervision in the community

incarcerated: confined in a jail or prison
