## Functionalist Perspective of Family

Functionalists see the family as an important institution that helps keep society stable. Each family role has responsibilities that support the well-being of both the family and society.

Sociologist George Murdock identified six key functions of the family from the functionalist perspective:

## Sexual regulation: defining normative or deviant relationships

## Reproduction: ensuring the continuation of society

Education (socialization): teaching children norms, values, and behaviors

Economic support: defining family as an economic unit where members share resources

Social class: the family unit helping children find a place in the stratification system

Intergenerational support: child-rearing and care of the sick and elderly helping to maintain social order and continuity across generations

Division of Labor

The functionalist perspective supports a gendered division of labor within families in which members take on different but complementary roles: instrumental roles (providing emotional support and caregiving). According to this perspective, separating these roles helps maintain balance and stability within the family system. When these roles change or shift, family members adjust their responsibilities to restore stability and ensure the family continues to function effectively.

## Critique of Functionalist Perspective

The most common critique of the functionalist perspective regarding marriage and family is that its conservative nature does not represent the realities of today's diverse family structures and the roles present in today's society. Additionally, the functionalist perspective tends to ignore the inequality and conflict that exist in marriages and the family (Ritzer 2019).

## Conflict Perspective of Family

The conflict perspective studies the family as an institution preserving a system of inequality and not as a cohesive unit. From this perspective, family life reflects larger inequalities found in society, and those inequalities are maintained through unequal access to resources and power across family units.

A key focus is how power is distributed among family members, especially because power is often tied to access to valued resources. Research shows that individuals with greater access to resources, such as money, tend to have more influence over family decision-making, meaning economic control can translate into greater authority within the household.

## Patterns of Gender Inequality

This power imbalance has often followed historical patterns of gender inequality. Historically, men were more likely to participate in paid labor, which gave them greater authority in many households. Conflict theorists connect this pattern to patriarchy, a system in which men hold more power than women. From this view, broader cultural inequality does not stop at the household door; it extends into family life and can shape who does what work, who has more say, and whose needs are prioritized.

## Critique of Conflict Perspective

Some power and resource imbalances are necessary at certain points in the life course, for example, between parents and young children. When children are young, it is practical for parents to assert authority to maintain safety and provide resources, ensuring children's needs are met. As children grow, the dynamics of power and resources can shift as both children and parents age, and it is important to examine the day-to-day interactions and meaning associated with those interactions.

Symbolic Interactionist Perspective of

## Family

Symbolic interactionists view family as a social construct, meaning its definition changes across time, culture, and place. Family provides a sense of belonging and shared identity. Through daily interactions, families pass on traditions and rituals, such as foods, stories, language, and celebrations, that shape identity and group membership.

Symbolic interactionists view the family as a key agent of socialization. Individuals learn how to communicate, behave, and understand social roles through interactions with parents and siblings. This includes gender role socialization, in which expectations about masculinity and femininity are learned through everyday experiences. For example, if a child grows up in a home where dad stays home and mom goes to work, they will associate the main roles of caregiving with dad and providing with mom.

## Meanings of Family Change Over Time

From a symbolic interactionist perspective, the roles of parent, mother, and father are based on shared meanings, and those meanings evolve over time. For example, in the past, the word parent was often assumed to mean a biological mother or father. Today, the meaning is broader and often reflects responsibility and caregiving rather than biology alone. A parent may include the following:

biological mother or father

adoptive parent

stepparent

guardian

grandparent or other adult raising a child
