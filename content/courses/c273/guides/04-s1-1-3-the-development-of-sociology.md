## The History of the Field of Sociology

Sociology emerged during a time of rapid social change in the 19th century. Industrialization transformed societies by moving large numbers of people from rural areas into cities, changing how people worked and lived. These changes created new social problems, such as overcrowding, poverty, and poor working conditions. As traditional ways of life were disrupted, scholars began to question how societies functioned and how social order could be maintained. Rather than relying on philosophy or tradition alone, these thinkers wanted to apply scientific methods to the study of society. This shift laid the foundation for sociology as a distinct field focused on understanding social patterns and social change.

## Key Figures in the Field of Sociology

Early sociologists believed that society could be studied systematically, much like the natural sciences. They were interested in identifying patterns in social life and understanding how social forces influenced behavior. These thinkers focused on questions such as the following:

- How do societies stay stable or change over time?
- What causes social conflict?
- How do social institutions shape people's lives?

Several early thinkers played important roles in shaping sociology. Each contributed ideas that influenced how society is studied today.

Auguste Comte (1798–1857)

Auguste Comte (1798–1857) Auguste Comte is considered the founder of sociology. He is credited with introducing the term sociology, which refers to the scientific study of society and social patterns. Comte argued that society could be studied scientifically, which he called positivism, and believed that identifying patterns in social life could help improve society. As the founder of sociology, Auguste Comte proposed applying the rigorous scientific method, used in the physical sciences, to study human society. He aimed to discover social laws based on evidence rather than speculation.

Harriet Martineau (1802–1876)

Harriet Martineau (1802–1876) Harriet Martineau helped introduce sociology to the English-speaking world by translating Auguste Comte’s work from French into English. She emphasized the importance of studying everyday life, specifically domestic life, including family roles and social inequality. Martineau believed sociologists should examine how social conditions affect different groups in unequal ways, particularly the lack of women’s rights.

Karl Marx (1818–1883)

Karl Marx (1818–1883) Karl Marx focused on social conflict, which refers to tension and struggle between groups with unequal access to power and resources. He argued that society is shaped by conflict between social classes, particularly between those who own resources and those who work for them. Marx’s ideas influenced the conflict approach, a sociological perspective that examines inequality and competition within society.

Herbert Spencer (1820–1903)

Herbert Spencer (1820–1903) Herbert Spencer applied ideas from biology to society and believed societies evolve over time. Social evolution refers to the idea that societies develop and change gradually through natural processes. Spencer argued that social institutions develop naturally and supported a hands-off approach to social change. Borrowing from Charles Darwin, Spencer said the concept of survival of the fittest applied to human society as well, stating that it was natural for some people to be poor and others to be rich. His ideas became known as Social Darwinism.

Émile Durkheim (1858–1917)

Émile Durkheim (1858–1917) Émile Durkheim emphasized the importance of social facts, which are patterns of behavior and ways of thinking that exist outside individuals and influence their actions. Examples of social facts include laws, norms, and shared values. Durkheim believed sociologists should study these patterns scientifically to understand how societies maintain order. His well-known book Suicide: A Study in Sociology highlighted how much society and social integration can influence even this most seemingly individual act. In 1897, Durkheim attempted to demonstrate the effectiveness of his rules of social research when he published Suicide: A Study in Sociology. Durkheim examined suicide statistics in different police districts to identify differences between Catholic and Protestant communities. Similar to many early sociologists, Durkheim researched differences between traditional and modern societies. He felt that people in both societies were connected with each other, but how they were connected differed. Mechanical solidarity was found in traditional societies in which people were connected by their shared beliefs, values, and similar work, allowing individuals to fill in for one another because their work tasks were alike.

Jane Addams (1860–1935)

Jane Addams (1860–1935) Jane Addams combined sociological research with social action, which involves using sociological knowledge to address social problems. Her research allowed her to advocate for immigrant rights, child labor laws, and women’s suffrage. She worked directly with communities to improve living and working conditions and believed understanding society required engagement with real people and real conditions.

Max Weber (1864–1920)

Max Weber (1864–1920) Max Weber is best known for his book The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, which analyzes the relationship between the economy and religion. Weber was interested in studying how economic transition led to rationalization and the development of bureaucratic organizations. Weber was critical of rationalization, believing it created an “Iron Cage” of systems in which people could become trapped in rigid bureaucratic processes.

W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963)

W.E.B. Du Bois (1868–1963) W.E.B. Du Bois, the first Black man to earn a doctorate from Harvard, studied race, inequality, and identity. He introduced the concept of double consciousness, which describes the experience of viewing oneself through multiple social identities at the same time, particularly in the context of race. Du Bois emphasized using sociology to understand and address social injustice, which contributed to the founding of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP).

## Other Important Contributors

The following is a list of other key figures and their contributions, which you will encounter in this course: Irving Janis studied groups and decision-making. He coined the term groupthink. George Mead developed the foundation of symbolic interactionism as well as the stages of development of the self, which focuses on how the self emerges through social experience, communication, and taking the role of the "other." Charles Horton Cooley introduced the looking-glass self, explaining that people develop their selfidentity by interpreting how others see and respond to them. Thorstein Veblen was known for his critique of capitalism and highlighted wasteful displays of wealth for status, called conspicuous consumption, and his theory of the leisure class (1899).
