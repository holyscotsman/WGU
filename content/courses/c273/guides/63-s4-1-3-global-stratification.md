What Is Global Stratification? Social stratification applies in the United States and across nations. This is referred to as global stratification, which is the unequal distribution of resources across nations. For example, some countries like the United States are highly industrialized and have wide access to education for most of the population, have strong infrastructures, and garner large amounts of global power. Other countries, such as Sierra Leone, are nonindustrialized and experience widespread poverty, limited access to education, and political instability.

## Global Classification of Stratification

Global classification methods are not only important in understanding economic differences among countries but also in providing ways to classify countries and identify trends in other areas. Different models and labels have been used by sociologists, economists, and scholars to classify global

inequality, which is the unequal distribution of resources between countries.

## Immanuel Wallerstein: World Systems Approach

Immanuel Wallerstein's (1979) world systems analysis uses an economic basis to understand global inequality spatially. Core countries are dominant capitalist countries. They are highly industrialized, technological, and urbanized. They house multinational corporations, which are based in one country but operate in others. Examples include the United States and the United Kingdom. Semi-Peripheral countries are between the core and peripheral countries. They are not powerful enough to dictate policy, but they nevertheless act as a major source of raw material and an expanding middle-class marketplace for core countries, while also exploiting peripheral countries. They often have large urban centers along with less-populated rural areas. An example includes Mexico. Peripheral countries have very little industrialization. Core countries typically colonized them, and their economies are, or were, structured mainly for the extraction of raw materials. That is, most of the factories and means of production are owned by core countries. According to Wallerstein's world systems analysis, core countries have an exploitative relationship with peripheral countries. Key takeaway: Sociologists, economists, and scholars use global classification models, such as Wallerstein's world systems approach, to understand global inequality by grouping countries into core, semi-peripheral, and peripheral classifications based on their levels of industrialization, economic power, and role in the global economy.

Key Terms global stratification: the unequal distribution of resources between countries global inequality: the concentration of resources in core nations and in the hands of a wealthy minority

gross domestic product (GDP): total value of goods and services produced by citizens of that country gross national income (GNI): the income of a nation calculated based on goods and services produced, plus income earned by citizens and corporations headquartered in that country life expectancy: the average period a person is expected to live under normal circumstances wealth: the total assets owned by a country and its population, less its debt Gini coefficient: a measure of income inequality within a country; can be used to compare one country's inequality to another's core country: dominant capitalist countries with more global power peripheral country: countries on the fringes of the global economy; exploited by core countries, with very little industrialization semi-peripheral country: in between core and peripheral countries; not powerful enough to dictate policy, but they act as a major source of raw materials and an expanding middle-class marketplace
