How Does Gender Inequality Develop?

Gender inequality starts with gender socialization in everyday interactions (for example, identifying one's unborn child as a boy or a girl and socializing them into toys or clothes that tell the world what their gender is before they have the cognitive ability to understand their identity).

Early introductions to gender often expose girls to nurturing stereotypes and boys to powerful or aggressive stereotypes. These are examples of gender stereotypes, which form the basis of sexism, including systems of oppression that value men over women.

When stereotypes influence how people are treated, they can lead to discrimination or unequal treatment based on group membership. Discrimination can occur in everyday interactions, but it can also become embedded in social institutions, a pattern sociologists refer to as institutional discrimination.

This visual illustrates the progression of gender inequality through several stages. It begins with gender socialization and moves through gender stereotypes, sexism, discrimination, and finally institutional discrimination. Each stage is represented with an icon and a brief explanation, showing how early social influences can lead to broader systemic inequality over time.

Key takeaway: Through socialization, children learn gender roles, stereotypes, and values early in life, which can lead to sexism and unequal treatment becoming embedded in everyday social life and institutions.

## Gender Stratification

How does gender inequality become structured? As gender expectations are learned early and reinforced through socialization and institutions, they do not remain limited to individual behavior. When these expectations are repeated across social institutions, they begin to shape access to resources, opportunities, and power for entire groups. Sociologists use the concept of gender stratification to describe this broader pattern of inequality among gender groups.

Gender stratification is the unequal distribution of wealth, power, and privilege based on gender. Rather than resulting from isolated acts of discrimination, gender stratification is embedded in social structures, influencing how institutions such as education, work, politics, and family life operate.

Key takeaway: Gender stratification occurs when learned gender expectations become embedded in social institutions, producing systematic inequalities in power, resources, and opportunities across society.

## The Second Shift

## The Second Shift

Women in the paid labor force also still do the majority of the unpaid work at home, a term sociologists call the second shift. On an average day, 84% of women (compared to 67% of men) spend time doing household management activities.

## The Gender Pay Gap

## The Gender Pay Gap

White women will generally make only 81 cents for every dollar made by their male counterparts.

The gender pay gap is starker for minorities. Black women earn about 63 cents, and Latinx women earn around 54 cents for every dollar men earn.

## The Motherhood Penalty

## The Motherhood Penalty

Part of the gender pay gap can be attributed to unique barriers faced by women regarding work experience and promotion opportunities.

A mother of young children is more likely to drop out of the labor force for several years or work on a reduced schedule than is the father.

In the United States, childless/childfree women with the same education and experience levels as men are typically paid with closer (but not exact) parity to men.

However, women with children are paid less.

## The Glass Ceiling

## The Glass Ceiling

The fact that women are typically unable to reach the executive suite is known as the glass ceiling. It is an invisible barrier that women encounter when trying to achieve positions in the highest levels of their jobs.

Women led 11% of Fortune 500 companies in 2025. This is the first time the number exceeded 10% (Fortune 2025).

Only five Black women have ever served as a Fortune 500 CEO, in the past 70 years (since the list was first published in 1955) (Forbes 2025).

The Glass Escalator

The Glass Escalator

Men, on the other hand, often experience the "glass escalator." The glass escalator is the faster, often hidden, promotion rates of men into management positions within traditionally female- dominated occupations. (Forbes, 2012) An example is seen in elementary school teaching, where principals typically rise from the ranks of teachers.

Elementary school leadership is a good example, where teachers are typically promoted to principals. Men constitute about 20% of all public elementary school teachers but account for about 44% of all elementary school principals (National Center for Education Statistics, 2009).

## Women in Politics

## Women in Politics

Historically, in the United States, political representation has been mostly made up of White men. The fact that the United States has never elected a female president demonstrates how deeply gender still shapes access to political power. The number of women elected to serve in Congress has increased over the years, but it does not yet accurately reflect the general population.

## Sexual Harassment

Another workplace problem is sexual harassment, which reflects broader patterns of gender inequality. Sexual harassment, as defined by federal guidelines and legal rulings and statutes, consists of unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, or physical conduct of a sexual nature used as a condition of employment or promotion (known as quid pro quo harassment) or that interferes with an individual's job performance and creates an intimidating or hostile environment (known as hostile work environment).

Why are Women Targeted?

Although men can be and are targeted, women are more often the targets of sexual harassment. This disparity is linked to gender socialization and cultural beliefs that depict women as sexual objects and encourage men to be sexually assertive. Together, these beliefs can lead some men to view verbal or physical advances in the workplace as acceptable.

## The Influence of Power and Structure

When these behaviors meet legal definitions, they constitute sexual harassment. This issue is also structural, reflecting the gendered nature of workplace power. Men in positions of authority may exploit their power over subordinate women, who may feel unable to resist for fear of retaliation, such as losing their jobs or missing promotion opportunities.

## Key

gender stereotypes: widely held beliefs or assumptions about the characteristics, behaviors, and roles appropriate for individuals based on their gender

gender socialization: the process through which individuals learn and internalize societal expectations and norms related to gender roles and behaviors

gender stratification: the unequal distribution of power, resources, and opportunities between genders in society

second shift: the unpaid labor, such as household tasks and caregiving, that many individuals (especially women) perform after completing paid work

gender pay gap: the difference in earnings between men and women, often expressed as a percentage of men's earnings

motherhood penalty: the economic disadvantages, including lower wages and fewer career opportunities, experienced by women with children

the glass ceiling: an invisible barrier that prevents individuals, especially women and minorities, from advancing to higher-level positions

the glass escalator: the advantage that men often experience in female-dominated professions, leading to faster advancement into higher positions

sexual harassment: unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, or other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature that affects employment or creates a hostile environment

quid pro quo: a form of sexual harassment in which employment benefits are conditioned upon the acceptance of sexual advances

hostile work environment: a workplace in which unwelcome conduct, including sexual harassment, creates an intimidating, offensive, or abusive atmosphere
