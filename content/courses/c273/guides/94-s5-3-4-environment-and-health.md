## Environmental Concerns

Human activities such as industrialization, fossil fuel use, and waste disposal affect the air we breathe, the water we drink, and the food we eat. These environmental exposures influence rates of asthma, cancer, infectious diseases, and chronic illness. Environmental sociology examines how social systems, inequality, and policy decisions shape environmental risks and, in turn, population health outcomes. For example, palm oil production in the tropics, where the expansion of transnational corporations' operations, assisted by favorable government policies, has functioned to displace indigenous people from their lands and rapidly accelerate tropical deforestation. The expansion of palm oil production is reshaping both society and the environment simultaneously. The following are environmental concerns and their impact on health.

## Water Pollution and Poor Sanitation

Climate Change (Global Warming) Climate Change (Global Warming) There is little to no doubt, at least among scientists, that global warming and more generally climate change are real phenomena with human-made causes. The burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas releases greenhouse gases like carbon dioxide into the atmosphere. These gases trap heat, changing global temperatures over time. Rising temperatures increase extreme weather events, heat-related illness, food insecurity, disease spread, rising sea levels, and higher mortality worldwide.

## Exploitation of Natural Resources

## Exploitation of Natural Resources

The overuse of nonrenewable resources (oil, natural gas, minerals, forests) and the unsustainable use of renewable resources (wind, water) increase environmental degradation and health risks. For example, deforestation is the permanent removal of forests and trees to make land available for other uses. This process reduces air quality, disrupts water systems, increases exposure to infectious diseases, and contributes to climate change.

## Agricultural Intensification and the Green Revolution

## Agricultural Intensification and the Green Revolution

Beginning in the 1940s and 1950s, the Green Revolution introduced high-yield crop varieties (HYVs) to increase food production in less developed nations. While it reduced famine in many regions, it also intensified resource use and environmental pressure.

## Sustainable Development

## Sustainable Development

Sustainable development refers to meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs, including a shift from nonrenewable to renewable energy sources and more environmentally responsible policies.

## Key Takeaway

## Key Takeaway

Environmental health problems such as air pollution, climate change, water contamination, and the exploitation of natural resources are driven by human activity and social systems, contributing to chronic disease, infectious illness, food insecurity, and global health disparities.

## Functionalist Perspective on Environment

The functionalist perspective considers pollution and other environmental problems to be an inevitable consequence of today's society, but it assumes that environmental problems that are too severe are certainly dysfunctional for society. Explanations include the following: Industrialization is a positive development for society, as it provides industries and jobs, among other benefits. Industrialization is a leading cause of accelerating global warming, which may cause societal dysfunctions if people are displaced, cannot work, or experience other consequences of environmental problems. Other concerns, such as population growth, have implications for the environment, such as depleting valuable resources and food supplies necessary for humans to survive and thrive.

Since functionalists are interested in the workings and interconnections of social institutions, they may also argue that it is a dysfunction of the institution of the state to allow corporations to engage in polluting practices that harm the environment. Key takeaway: From a functionalist perspective, environmental problems such as pollution and climate change are unintended consequences of industrialization and population growth.

## Conflict Perspective on Environment

From the conflict perspective, the world's environmental problems are not inevitable and instead arise from three related sources and reflect decisions by political elites and multinational corporations. The three sources are as follows: pollution by multinational corporations that occurs due to weak regulations failure to enforce the regulations that do exist weak regulations on ultra-processed foods (UPFs) negatively affecting health Key takeaway: From a conflict perspective, environmental problems result from decisions by political elites and multinational corporations, where weak regulations and poor enforcement allow pollution and environmental harm.

Symbolic interactionism offers four types of explanations of environmental problems. Why do people engage (or not engage) in environmentally sustainable behavior? examines why individuals participate in activities like recycling or reducing pollution explores reasons people choose not to act and barriers to involvement suggests that understanding behavior is key to solving environmental problems How do people perceive environmental problems? focuses on public attitudes toward environmental issues, such as pollution

recognizes that beliefs shape whether problems are taken as truth and taken seriously how the media shapes beliefs and perceptions of environmental effects emphasizes that policy efforts depend on public opinion suggests solutions must address how people understand the issue How do problems become socially constructed? Environmental problems become social problems when enough people and organizations recognize them as harmful. Examples include recycling programs to reduce pollution or ensuring healthy drinking water. Scientific evidence alone does not always lead to action. Public pressure and advocacy groups often influence change. How do different groups interpret problems differently? Social background influences environmental beliefs. Cultural differences shape how risks are understood. Communities may disagree about the seriousness of environmental threats. Interpretation depends on social position and lived experience. Key takeaway: From a symbolic interactionist perspective, pollution and environmental problems are shaped by everyday behaviors, social norms, and cultural values, meaning that individual actions and shared beliefs influence whether environmental change occurs.
