## Defining Health, Medicine, and Illness

Medical sociology is one of the largest subsections of sociology, focusing on the "social causes and consequences of health and illness" (Cockerham 2007) as well as the delivery of healthcare (Ritzer 2019). Within the study of medicine, sociologists recognize two related branches: Sociology in medicine: Research that focuses on questions useful to medical professionals and the practice of medicine. Sociology of medicine: Research that examines health, illness, and medical systems as subjects of sociological analysis.

## Definitions

Sociologists use the following core terms and definitions to analyze how societies define, experience, and respond to health and illness.

## Social Epidemiology

In addition to studying healthcare institutions, sociologists examine how health and disease are distributed across populations. Epidemiology is the study of the distribution, patterns, and causes of disease within populations. Social epidemiology focuses specifically on how these patterns of health outcomes differ according to social factors: social class, age, gender, and race and ethnicity.

## Social Class

## Social Class

Socioeconomic status (often measured by income, education, and occupation) strongly influences health outcomes. Individuals with higher socioeconomic status generally experience longer life expectancy and better overall health. Early-life conditions: Childhood conditions can shape long-term health. Poor nutrition or exposure to environmental hazards during childhood can increase the risk of chronic diseases later in life. Living environment: Lower-income communities are more likely to be located near pollution sources such as highways or industrial areas. These environments can increase exposure to harmful pollutants and limit safe spaces for physical activity. Food access and nutrition: Many low-income neighborhoods have limited access to grocery stores with affordable fresh foods. As a result, residents may rely more on processed or fast foods, which can contribute to poor health outcomes. Stress and healthcare access: Financial instability and limited resources can create chronic stress that negatively affects physical health. Lower-income families may also face barriers to accessing consistent preventive and routine healthcare.

## Age

## Age

Age influences healthcare needs, health risks, and healthcare spending across the lifespan. Medical costs tend to be highest during infancy and again in older adulthood as individuals experience increased medical needs and chronic conditions. In addition, an aging population explosion due to the baby boomer generation is putting a strain on the healthcare workforce. Changing health needs across the lifespan: Healthcare needs evolve over time. Infants often require intensive care early in life, while older adults are more likely to manage chronic diseases that require ongoing treatment. Healthcare spending by age: Healthcare spending increases significantly in later life. Older adults account for a large share of medical expenditures because they often require more frequent and complex care. Aging population trends: The proportion of older adults in the United States is increasing. This shift is expected to increase demand for healthcare services and long-term care in the coming decades. Healthcare workforce and system strain: An aging population explosion may place additional pressure on healthcare systems. Some projections suggest that physician shortages could occur as demand for care rises. Representation in medical research: Children and older adults have historically been underrepresented in clinical trials. Limited inclusion can result in treatments that are not fully tested for these age groups.

## Gender

## Gender

Gender can influence how diseases are studied, diagnosed, and treated. Historically, medical research and clinical guidelines have often been based on male participants, shaping how symptoms and treatments are understood. Historical bias in medical research: Many early medical studies relied primarily on male research participants. As a result, medical knowledge about disease symptoms and treatments was often centered on male physiology. Differences in disease presentation: Men and women may experience different symptoms for the same condition. When medical guidelines focus mainly on one set of symptoms, illnesses in other groups may be overlooked. For example, heart disease is the leading cause of death among women in the United States. However, women frequently experience symptoms such as jaw, neck, or back pain rather than chest pain, which is commonly reported by men. Implications for diagnosis and treatment: Because women's symptoms may differ from traditional descriptions, their heart attacks are sometimes missed or diagnosed later. Delayed recognition can lead to delays in treatment and poorer health outcomes.

## Race and Ethnicity

## Race and Ethnicity

Race and ethnicity are associated with differences in health outcomes in the United States. These disparities reflect a combination of social conditions, access to resources, and broader structural factors that affect health. Differences in disease and mortality rates: Some racial and ethnic minority groups experience higher rates of certain illnesses and mortality. For example, African Americans have higher rates of several chronic diseases and infant mortality. Infant mortality disparities: Infant mortality rates vary across racial and ethnic groups in the United States. African American infants experience significantly higher mortality rates compared with the national average. Role of socioeconomic factors: Socioeconomic status contributes to some health differences across racial and ethnic groups. However, disparities persist even when individuals have similar income or education levels. Structural and social determinants: Factors such as unequal access to healthcare, environmental exposures, and the stress associated with discrimination can influence health outcomes. These conditions contribute to long-term differences in disease risk. Variation across groups: Health outcomes differ among racial and ethnic populations. Some groups, such as Hispanic populations, demonstrate lower mortality rates than expected given their socioeconomic status, a pattern sometimes called the Hispanic paradox.

## Social Factors and Determinants of Health

Social factors such as social class, age, gender, and race or ethnicity help explain why health outcomes differ across populations. In addition to these social influences, behavioral determinants of health, such as substance use, sexual practices, and diet, contribute to major health outcomes. Health and health behaviors are determined by influences at multiple levels, including personal (i.e., biological, psychological), organizational/institutional, environmental (i.e., both social and physical), and policy levels.
