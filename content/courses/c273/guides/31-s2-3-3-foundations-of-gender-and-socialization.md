## Sex and Gender

Before discussing how socialization influences gender, it is important to make a clear distinction between these terms.

## Defining Sex

Sex refers to biological characteristics, such as chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive anatomy, that classify people as male or female. Because biological traits determine sex, it does not vary significantly across cultures.

## Defining Gender

In contrast, gender is socially constructed. (Socially constructed means people in a society agree on its meaning and expectations, not because it is biologically fixed or natural.) Gender refers to behaviors, expectations, and identities that society attributes to being feminine or masculine. Gender is about how societies interpret those biological traits and build norms around them.

Unlike sex, gender varies across cultures and historical periods. What is considered masculine or feminine in one society may be understood very differently in another society. It is helpful to think of gender as a spectrum, ranging from feminine to masculine characteristics. No one is completely feminine or masculine all of the time.

Key takeaway: Sex refers to biological characteristics, while gender refers to socially constructed roles, behaviors, and expectations that societies assign to people based on perceived sex.

## Gender Roles and Gender Socialization

Societies attach expectations to gender categories through gender roles. Gender roles are society's concept of how men and women are expected to look and how they should behave. These expectations influence everyday behavior, including clothing choices, emotional expression, career paths, and family responsibilities. From a young age, individuals learn which behaviors are rewarded or discouraged based on perceived gender.

The process through which people learn these expectations of gender roles is known as gender socialization. Gender socialization teaches individuals how to act, speak, and interact in ways that align with cultural definitions of masculinity and femininity. For example, parents started gender socialization before birth through increased participation in "gender reveal" parties in the United States in the 2000s.

## Influence of Agents of Socialization on Gender Roles

Gender socialization occurs through the agents of socialization: family, education, peer groups, and mass media. Families often provide the earliest messages about gender, while schools, peers, and media reinforce and reproduce gender expectations over time. Through repeated exposure to these agents, gender roles

come to feel natural, even though they are socially created.

Key takeaway: Sex is based on biology, while gender is shaped by social norms and learned through gender socialization. The family typically provides the first messages about gender roles.

## Gender Roles Across Cultures

Gender roles are not the same across all societies and are shaped by cultural norms and values rather than biology alone. The following are key points related to gender roles across cultures:

The view that someone is either a man/boy or a woman/girl is specific to certain cultures and is not universal. In some cultures, gender is viewed as fluid.

Some societies recognize more than two gender categories. For example, in Samoan culture, fa' afafine are individuals who are born biologically male but embody both masculine and feminine traits and are considered a valued part of society.

Cross-cultural research shows that behaviors, clothing, and work associated with masculinity or femininity vary widely between societies.

## Key

sex: biological characteristics, such as chromosomes, hormones, and reproductive anatomy, that classify people as male or female

gender: socially constructed meanings, behaviors, roles, and expectations that societies associate with being masculine and feminine

gender roles: social expectations about how people of different genders should behave, appear, and interact

gender socialization: the process through which individuals learn gender roles and expectations through interaction with family, peers, institutions, and the media
