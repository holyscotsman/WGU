## The Looking-Glass Self

While George Herbert Mead focused on how the self develops through stages of interaction, sociologist Charles Horton Cooley emphasized how the self is shaped by social feedback. Cooley argued that individuals do not form a sense of self in isolation. Instead, people's self-understanding is constructed, in part, by their perception of how others view them. He called this process the looking- glass self.

The looking-glass self explains how individuals evaluate themselves based on how they imagine others perceive them. This process happens continuously in everyday interactions and does not require direct feedback from others.

## Cooley's Three Parts of the Looking-Glass Self

According to Cooley, the looking-glass self explains how we come to see ourselves by doing the following:

1. imagining how we appear to others 2. imagining how others judge us 3. reacting emotionally to that imagined judgment

The process of the looking-glass self has the following outcomes:

Self-image: Self-image is how a person sees themselves in a particular situation or context. It is directly influenced by the looking-glass self because it is situational and flexible. How you think others see you in a specific moment (a presentation, a date, a class discussion) can immediately change your self-image.

Self-concept: Self-concept is a person's overall, stable understanding of who they are, including their traits, abilities, and values. It develops over time through many repeated looking-glass experiences. It is more stable and reflects patterns rather than single interactions.

## Example: The Looking-Glass Self in the Workplace

Imagine an employee giving a presentation during a team meeting. While speaking, the employee notices a colleague checking their phone and another colleague taking notes.

First, the employee imagines how they appear to others, thinking they may seem unprepared or unclear. Next, the employee imagines how others are judging that appearance, assuming the colleague on the phone is bored and that the notes reflect criticism rather than interest. Finally, the employee reacts to that imagined judgment by feeling anxious and speeding through the rest of the presentation.

Even though the colleagues may simply be multitasking or engaged, the employee's interpretation of others' reactions shapes how they feel and behave. This example illustrates how the looking-glass self operates in everyday workplace interactions based on perceived, not actual, judgments.

Key takeaway: The looking-glass self explains how individuals develop a sense of self by imagining how others see and judge them and then responding emotionally to those perceived judgments, shaping both self-image in specific situations and self-concept over time.

## Key

social feedback: real or imagined reactions from others that influence how individuals evaluate themselves and adjust behavior

looking-glass self: the process through which individuals develop a sense of self by imagining how they appear to others and how others judge them and reacting emotionally to those perceived judgments

self-image: how a person sees themselves in a specific situation or context, which can change depending on social interactions and perceived feedback

self-concept: a person's overall and relatively stable understanding of who they are, formed over time through repeated social interactions and experiences
