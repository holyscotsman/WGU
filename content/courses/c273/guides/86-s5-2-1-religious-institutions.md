What Is Religion? Some people associate religion with places of worship (e.g., a synagogue or church), others with a ritual or experience (e.g., confession or meditation), and still others with beliefs that guide their daily lives. All these examples define religion, which is a system of beliefs, values, and practices concerning what a person holds sacred or considers to be spiritually significant.

## Element

## Definition

## Beliefs

Shared ideas about what is sacred or spiritually significant, including beliefs about God or Gods, morality, life, and the afterlife

## Rituals

Rituals or expected behaviors, such as prayer, worship services, fasting, confession, meditation, weddings, or funerals, that express and reinforce shared beliefs and values

## Religion as a Social Institution

While some people think of religion as something individual because religious beliefs can be highly personal, religion is also a social institution. In countries where the government has banned religion, the state ideology often functions like a religion, supplying the shared beliefs, values, and rituals that traditional religion would otherwise provide. Official doctrines become moral frameworks, national heroes replace holy figures, and public ceremonies mirror religious rites meant to reinforce unity and loyalty. Key takeaway: Religion is a social institution made up of shared beliefs, values, and practices centered on what is considered sacred, and it exists in every society as a cultural universal that shapes meaning, behavior, and community life.

## World Religions

While religion can be defined broadly as a social institution and system of meaning, religions around the world vary in their beliefs, rituals, and cultural traditions. Major world religions developed in different regions of the world, but each has shaped social life and cultural practices in lasting ways. These religions influence how people celebrate holidays, organize families, follow moral rules, and participate in community life. Although beliefs differ, religion often guides daily behavior and cultural traditions. ## Religious Organizations

Sociologists often describe religious organizations along a continuum, ranging from small, counternormative groups to large, culturally established institutions.

Select each organization below to learn more.

## Collapse all

## Cult

## Cult

The smallest, most counter-normative religious organizations are cults, more frequently referred to as new religious movements. Most sociologists avoid the term cult to avoid the negative connotations associated with specific groups using the term cult but were not in fact religious organizations, such as the Manson Cult (1969) or Heaven's Gate (1997)(Ritzer 2019). Cults often begin as small groups seeking growth and influence. If membership expands and beliefs become more culturally accepted, they eventually become a church, which is the largest level of religious organization.

## Church

## Church

A church is large, normalized in its society, and bureaucratically organized. It often has a governing body that instructs its congregations in practice and belief. Note: In sociology, the term church refers to a type of religious organization, not a building or general religion.

## Sect

## Sect

A sect is a smaller nonbureaucratic group that has dissatisfaction with the beliefs and practices of the larger group (often dissatisfaction with how religious life is being lived). Sects form when people break away from an established religious organization in search of a more authentic religious experience. Examples include Amish, Hutterites within Christianity, Hasidic Jews within Judaism, and Alawites or Isma'ilis within Islam. Sects are often anti-establishment, nonbureaucratic, and nonhierarchical, and they are often led by laypersons without formal training. They emphasize religious purity and require strong commitment and adherence to group norms.

## Denominations

## Denominations

As a church grows and gains new members, some might break into denominations or independent branches. For example, Judaism includes several denominations, such as Orthodox, Conservative, and Reform Judaism, which differ in how they interpret Jewish law, religious traditions, and the role of religion in modern life. Denominations differ from sects in many fundamental ways. For example, denominations within a church share their core institutional beliefs. Sects, on the other hand, are groups that break away from churches precisely because they differ in monumental ways in either beliefs or practices.
