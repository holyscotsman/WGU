# 2.4 Productivity

### Essential Question

What is productivity, and how does it improve an organization's efficiency and competitiveness?

Productivity "The term productivity has been recognized for its contribution to operational, organizational, industrial, and national competitiveness" (Ojha, 2014). Productivity is a mathematical calculation; it is the ratio of the outputs achieved divided by the inputs consumed to achieve those outputs.

## Productivity = Output/Input

Most operations have more than one input and more than one output. Economically, the inputs are labor by managers and workers (either internally or externally) capital for land, facilities, and equipment materials, including energy requirements As productivity increases, organizations can do the same work with less effort or can do more work with the same effort. Increases in productivity reduce costs, lower prices, and provide a basis for competing in world markets. While productivity is defined by a mathematical equation, efficiency is a general descriptor of the time or effort needed to do work. Efficiency is often used to mean achieving an outcome with a minimum amount of effort; that is, mitigating waste. In this way, efficiency addresses the same challenges as productivity, which is a precise measure. Productivity improvements are beneficial to the organization, to management, to consumers, and to workers. In any situation, there are limits on resources, capital, equipment, material, energy, and labor. Additionally, none of these resources are free. If an organization can produce more products with fewer resources while improving quality, it will achieve two significant advantages. First, the unit cost of the product will decline because less labor or fewer materials are required to produce each unit. This, in turn, makes the product cost less and the company more price competitive. Second, there will be unused resources that can be employed to develop and produce new products and enhance existing products.

## Productivity Revolutions

When significant increases in productivity have been achieved, revolutionary changes in resource allocation have occurred. These types of shifts in resource allocation have changed the face of economic development in the United States three times.

## The First Revolution

Increases in farming productivity resulting from the development of better methods and machinery caused farm labor in the United States to decline from 95% in the early 1800s to less than 3% today.

## The Second Revolution

During the late 1800s and the first half of the 1900s, improvements in manufacturing productivity freed resources for the rapid expansion of service operations. As manufacturers invested in equipment to automate production, less labor was needed to make the same amount of finished goods. Banking, healthcare, insurance, retail, and other service industries grew because of the labor freed by mechanizing operations.

## The Third Revolution

The next wave of productivity improvements began in the 1950s with the development and commercial application of computers. This phase, which some call the post-industrial era, began with large, difficult-to-operate mainframe computers used by governments and a few very large businesses. The revolution progressed through large-scale databases, telecommunications technology, the personal computer, the internet, and smart devices.

## Productivity and Improved Living

## Standards

Productivity measures the ability to produce goods and services (outputs) compared to the inputs or resources used in the process. Most work in the economy is done by groups of people working in organizations. As discussed earlier in this section, organizations exist to meet the needs of society that people working alone cannot. It is through these organizations that people achieve the cooperation and coordination to produce the array of services and goods consumed each day. Organizations work in the following ways: First, organizations allow individuals to specialize in work, such as production, engineering, and sales. Second, organizations support the development and implementation of technology and automation to achieve greater productivity. Third, organizations provide a mechanism for coordinating work toward a common set of goals. Examining and redesigning organizational processes and activities is a key source of productivity improvement. The following examples illustrate these points. Specialization: A good example of specialization is in life insurance. Product design for life insurance requires an estimate of life expectancy. This effort is critical to setting the terms and conditions of the policy, including the premium. Actuaries are statisticians who specialize in making this estimate. Their productivity (i.e., ability to make the estimate quickly and accurately) is greatly enhanced by specialization. Technology and Automation: With the capability to send and receive money electronically, standing in line in a cashier's office or bank is no longer necessary. Advances in technology and automation present opportunities to cut costs and reduce processing time while increasing valueadded work.

Process Redesign—Changing the process so that the customer can communicate directly with the people doing the work increases the productivity of all participants. It can also be done by sharing feedback from customers in a video format and providing "made by" information so that the customers know who made the product. Providing customers with the employee's email or other points of contact is helpful, as well.
