# 4.1 Inventory Management Models

### Essential Question

How do economic order quantity (EOQ), economic production quantity (EPQ), and quantity discounts model (QDM) impact inventory costs?

## Inventory Costs

Inventory decisions involve trade-offs: Keeping more inventory may decrease the amount of stockouts as well as reduce the ordering and setup costs, but keeping more inventory requires more investment, storage, and management costs.

Of the four inventory costs, organizations primarily consider ordering costs, carrying costs, and stockout costs when deciding on an appropriate inventory management model. Purchase cost is considered in the quantity discount model (QDM), which will be discussed later in this lesson.

One major component of cost associated with inventory is the cost of replenishment or ordering cost. If a part or raw material is ordered from outside suppliers, an ordering cost is incurred. Conversely, parts, subassemblies, or finished products may be produced in-house. In that case, ordering cost is represented by the costs associated with changing over equipment from producing one item to producing another and is referred to as setup cost. This lesson will refer to both ordering costs and setup costs as ordering costs.

## Carrying (Holding) Costs

Although it costs money to replenish inventory, it also costs money to carry that inventory. Such inventory carrying costs, also called holding costs, may include costs paid for storage space, interest paid on borrowed money to finance the inventory, and any losses incurred due to damage or obsolescence.

## Stockout Costs

When the demand during the lead time to replenish an inventory item is greater than planned, a stockout can occur. The stockout cost can be associated with the cost of a backorder, a lost sale, and even a lost customer. These costs can be reduced by carrying extra inventory to protect against the possibility of a stockout. Organizations must determine which items are more susceptible to stockouts to plan safety stock levels effectively.

## Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) Model

To help reduce inventory costs, organizations use different item ordering methods to determine the optimum order quantity. The economic order quantity (EOQ) model is one method concerned primarily with the cost of ordering and the cost of carrying inventory, but the basic model can be expanded to address several issues. The goal of firms using the EOQ model is to minimize the total annual costs of ordering and carrying inventory by determining a fixed order quantity.

Ordering cost and carrying cost can be described with the analogy of two children sitting on a seesaw. When one goes up, the other goes down, and vice versa. This trade-off appears to present somewhat of a quandary: If an effort is made to decrease total annual carrying costs, then total annual ordering costs will increase—and vice versa. A solution to this dilemma is to combine the two costs as total costs and minimize only that cost. As the following figure indicates, there is just one point at which total costs are minimized. This point is where the ordering costs and the carrying costs are equal. The order quantity associated with that point is called the economic order quantity (EOQ).

## Assumptions of EOQ

The EOQ model is based upon a set of assumptions that provide a simplification of real ordering processes. Those assumptions are described here. Later, variations of the EOQ model will be considered when some of these assumptions are not necessary.

Constant known demand: The first assumption is that demand is fairly stable, or constant, and reasonably known.

Cost per unit is not dependent on order quantity: Most things can be purchased at a lower cost per unit if they are purchased in larger quantities. For instance, large sizes of laundry detergent usually cost less per ounce than smaller sizes. This example, however, makes the purchase cost a variable cost—something for which the EOQ model does not account. Thus, the assumption is that the purchase cost per unit remains the same, regardless of whether the amount is one, 100, or 1,000 units each time.

Entire order delivered at once: This assumption relates to how inventory is replenished. Gradually building up inventory, as would happen in a clothing factory, is one possibility. As a particular model of the jacket is produced, the inventory of that jacket builds up gradually. Another possibility is for all units in an order to arrive at one time, which is what happens when a retail store orders from a factory. The factory ships an entire order of the jacket at one time, and the store's inventory is replenished all at once. It is this instantaneous replenishment that is assumed by the EOQ. Ordering costs and carrying costs are known and independent: The final assumption is that the costs of ordering and carrying inventory are known. In many cases, such costs can be determined from company records or from the accounting department; however, the costs are sometimes not readily available and must be estimated. Also, the assumption is made that these two costs are not related in any way.

These assumptions are generally appropriate for independent demand items such as finished goods whose demand is fairly uniform. These assumptions may seem restrictive and possibly unrealistic. Recall, however, that the EOQ model is the basic starting point. This model may be altered to relax some of these assumptions and more closely match reality.

## Quantity Discount Model (QDM)

Items usually cost less per unit when purchased in bulk. Therefore, the order quantity directly influences the purchase price of an item. This is called the quantity discount model (QDM).

Starting with the basic EOQ model, it is possible to develop other models that have less restrictive assumptions or that are appropriate for other situations. One assumption from the example is that the purchase price remains the same regardless of how many units are purchased. In reality, this is usually not true. For example, things usually cost less per unit when purchased by the case rather than individually. An entire truckload of an item will generally cost less than if the same quantity was purchased case by case. Order quantities directly influence the purchase price of an item.

Incorporating quantity discounts into the order quantity calculations requires modification of the total cost calculation to include purchase cost. The total annual cost of purchasing an item may vary due to quantity discounts. The optimal answer cannot be found by substituting numbers into a formula, as in the example for the EOQ.

## Economic Production Quantity (EPQ)

Like the economic order quantity (EOQ), the economic production quantity (EPQ) helps companies control the cost of ordering, producing, and holding inventory for manufactured items. The difference between the two concepts lies in their assumptions and their applications. Whereas the EOQ assumes that inventory will arrive complete (in one shipment, for example), the EPQ allows for incremental ordering and depletion. Therefore, EPQ is commonly used for production items where inventory is manufactured as a component part, placed in the company's warehouse storage, and later sent out into a production process. EPQ assumes that an organization's capacity to produce a product exceeds its demand. To determine EPQ, it is important to note that the ordering cost is considered the setup cost required to change over the manufacturing equipment.

## Fixed Interval Model

The EOQ model is most often used when a company can continuously monitor its inventory level to determine when the order point is reached. Continuous monitoring may not be reasonable or necessary. In such cases, orders are often placed at a fixed interval of time, as in a periodic review system. In spite of this difference, the EOQ model can be used to determine an optimum interval for reviewing and replenishing inventory. From any given review interval, a policy can be developed to determine how much to order.

## Review Interval

The time between reviews, or the review interval, will determine average order quantity because the annual demand must be met during one year. If orders are placed frequently, then each order will be smaller. Infrequent ordering means larger lot sizes.

Recall, though, that there is still a trade-off between ordering costs and carrying costs. Thus, the review interval that is chosen can directly affect total costs.

## Target Level

In a periodic review system, the inventory level at the time of review will vary, as depicted in the following figure. If a constant amount was ordered each time, it would be very difficult to recover from a low inventory level. Instead, the quantity ordered must increase inventory up to a level sufficient to cover anticipated demand before the next order is received. This is called the target level.

Since a periodic review system only reviews inventory at the review interval, once an order has been placed, the inventory will not be checked again until the next review point. Each order must contain a sufficient amount to cover expected demand during the review interval plus demand during the lead time, or a stockout will occur. Because of the nature of periodic review systems, safety stock is likely more important, and the quantity will be larger.
