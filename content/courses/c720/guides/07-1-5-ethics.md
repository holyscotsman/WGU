# 1.5 Ethics

### Essential Question

What are some challenges of ethical and social responsibilities for sustainable operations?

## Legal, Environmental, and Ethical Issues

Today, managers and organizations are facing a staggering array of problems that their predecessors largely ignored. The managers of the past often viewed labor relations and worker safety, environmental pollution, and product safety as outside their area of concern. They often made decisions that were in the best interest of the organization's stockholders but that were detrimental to labor, to the environment, and even to the customer.

As a result of this neglect, organizations faced labor unions, environmental advocates, and consumer groups that have been successful in passing legislation to define labor-management relations, regulate the safety of the workplace, shape environmental and sustainability programs, and protect consumers. Government, therefore, now plays a significant role in regulating organizations and their operations. Companies must learn to regulate themselves to a higher degree to avoid burdensome government regulations. Companies operating globally must understand the legislative and ethical requirements for competing in each country.

Each of these areas of concern is related to operations. Normally, operations have the most workers to build the products consumers purchase, and they are often the focus of union activity. Additionally, for non-service production organizations, operations are the place where workers can be injured, and they have the greatest potential for creating environmental pollution. Organizations must develop standards for ethical behavior that meet community expectations. The standards of ethical behavior encompass product safety during and after production and how the product affects the environment. It is also reasonable to expect those standards to increase as the standard of living increases. Product safety issues, including protections for important ecosystems, are more prominent, better understood, and better managed in a developed country with more resources than in an undeveloped or underdeveloped country.

Today, managers need to search for solutions where all the stakeholders—stockholders, management, labor, consumers, and the general public—can benefit. Fortunately, the new generation of managers has recognized the need to develop these approaches, and some impressive strides have been made.

Ethical Behavior

Ethics are a sense of what is right and wrong that guides behavior. This set of standards is often more stringent than legal standards. For example, one of the greatest successes for any company to choose ethics over profits was Johnson & Johnson and the Tylenol case from 1982. Johnson & Johnson removed every Tylenol bottle in the United States when the concern was specifically product tampering in the Chicago area. This could have crushed Johnson & Johnson, but they chose ethics over profit.

Read " Ethical Reasoning in Business " from Religion & Liberty.

Ethical behavior is a corporate issue that affects the company's bottom line, like with Johnson & Johnson. The answer is complex, but it can be partially understood by reviewing earlier points on labor relations, the environment, and the customer. Some organizations have attempted to maximize short-term earnings to the stockholder per share by cutting corners on plant safety or environmental compliance. Usually, these actions have separated management from labor, the consumer, and the general public.
