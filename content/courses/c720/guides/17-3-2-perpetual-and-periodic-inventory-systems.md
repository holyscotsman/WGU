# 3.2 Perpetual and Periodic Inventory Systems

### Essential Question

What are the advantages and disadvantages of perpetual and periodic inventory systems?

## Perpetual Inventory Systems

A perpetual inventory system continuously monitors inventory levels. It is also known as a continuous review system. Under such a system, inventory transactions are recorded as they occur. Computerbased systems have made the transaction process much easier and faster for a larger number of items. For example, grocery stores and retailers use point-of-sale (POS) systems that record the transaction as each item is read by the barcode scanner. Retailers pass this information along to the supplier so that replenishment can take place. An ATM is another example of a perpetual inventory system; this system updates the balance in a patron's bank account as withdrawals and deposits are made. Debit cards are one way for the bank to move the money from the user's account for a purchase. These cards also allow the bank to determine if there is sufficient money to cover the transaction. Companies use barcodes for raw materials and work-in-process inventories as a way to computerize these inventory records. The barcodes are used on a component part ordered from a supplier. Using barcodes or other similar technologies allows the firm to be constantly aware of inventory levels. When inventory drops to a predetermined reorder point, an order for more items is generated. This ordering is completed automatically by the same computer system that maintains the inventory records. For the continuous review system to work, it is necessary to know the inventory level constantly and to have a supplier who will replenish inventory at any time. In this way, when the order point in the continuous review system is reached, an order can be sent, and delivery can take place.

## Periodic Inventory Systems

When a company does not track the level of inventory continuously or a supplier only delivers at specific intervals, such as monthly or weekly, the periodic review system can be used. The periodic review system is designed to place an order only when on-hand inventory information is available and within the supplier's specific delivery intervals. The storage tanks at most gasoline service stations are refilled on a preset schedule—for example, once a week on Friday. Monitoring inventory continuously and placing an order on Saturday does not help because the delivery will not be made until the following Friday. A predetermined target inventory level must be set that will allow the company to meet expected demand based on the order interval period and the expected demand during the replenishment leadtime interval. If the on-hand inventory is less than the target level, an order is generated that will push the on-hand inventory back to the predetermined level. The periodic review system, also called the fixed interval system, can be run without a computerized system to monitor inventory levels, but it is more likely to run out of stock because the system is unable to react quickly to sudden changes in demand. To minimize the chance of stockouts (situations in which orders cannot be filled), the firm must increase the level of safety stock inventory.

## ABC Analysis and Classification

Many organizations use both perpetual and periodic inventory systems simultaneously. The reasoning might depend on the priority of specific inventory items that require continuous updates versus other inventory items that are not as important. Companies do not need to keep accurate track of all inventory items. For instance, certain parts may have a relatively low value and be used infrequently; those items can often be monitored very loosely. On the other hand, high-value and high-usage items must be tracked carefully and continuously. ABC analysis was developed to determine which inventory items should receive the highest level of control. By multiplying the dollar value of each item by its annual usage, a dollar usage value can be obtained for each item. Dollar usage follows the Pareto principle in that only 20% of all the items account for 80% of the total dollar usage, while the remaining items account for only 20% of the total dollar usage. This principle leads to the ABC classification, which is based on focusing inventory control efforts where the payoff is highest. After calculating the dollar usage for each inventory item, the items are ranked by dollar usage from highest to lowest. The first 20% of the items that represent 80% of the total dollar usage are classified as A items. These are the expensive items that warrant the closest control and monitoring through a perpetual inventory system. Accurate inventory records are very important, and the potential for cutting costs through careful buying and close scrutiny of safety stocks is high. The next 30% of items are classified as B items and represent 15 % of the total dollar usage. These are items of moderate value and receive moderate control. Finally, the last 50% of stocked items are classified as C items. These low-cost inventory items account for the remaining 5% of the total dollar usage and can be monitored loosely, with larger safety stocks maintained to avoid stockouts.
